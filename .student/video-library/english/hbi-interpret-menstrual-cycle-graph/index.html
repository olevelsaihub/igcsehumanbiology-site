Read the narration

A menstrual cycle lasts about 28 days. Day 1 is the start of menstruation: the uterus lining breaks down and leaves the body, for about 5 days. Then the lining grows thicker. Ovulation, the release of an egg, is about day 14. After that the lining is kept thick. Hormones control this. In this made-up sketch, oestrogen rises in the first half and makes the lining grow. A surge of a hormone called LH causes ovulation. Then progesterone rises and keeps the lining thick.

Step one: read the axes and the key first. Many of these graphs have two y-axes, with hormone on the left and lining width on the right. Check which line uses which axis. Step two: find the days asked for. Draw a ruler line up from day 4 and from day 16, then across to the axis. In these made-up values, the width goes from 0.5 to 3.5, an increase of three millimetres. Step three: name the hormone and link it to a purpose. Oestrogen makes the lining thicker. Progesterone keeps it thick. Fertilisation is when a sperm joins with the egg. It grows into an embryo. To implant means the embryo sinks into the thick lining. The lining's blood vessels give it food and oxygen. Step four: if the egg is fertilised, progesterone stays high. In this made-up sketch, the lining line stays high and flat, so no menstruation. Without fertilisation, it falls. Oestrogen would also level off, not climb to a new peak.

Now a real exam question. The graph shows lining width and oestrogen in one cycle. Its key says the dashed line is oestrogen: always go by the key. Part i asked for the change in lining width between day 6 and day 14. Part ii asks you to explain the importance of this change. Three marks. First, what is the change? Use the lining line and the right axis. On day 6 it is 0.625 millimetres. On day 14 it is 4.25. So the lining width shows an increase of about 3.6 millimetres. Second, when does it matter? Only if the egg is fertilised after ovulation and becomes an embryo. The lining gets ready in case that happens. Third, why? The thick lining lets the embryo implant in the uterus wall. Its blood vessels supply food and oxygen. Here is the full answer. This answer is by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme.

A common mistake is reading the lining from the hormone axis. Follow the lining line to its own axis, the one in millimetres. Another mistake is describing only the shape of the line, like 'it goes up'. Say why it matters: a thick lining is ready for implantation. Last, menstruation is not when the lining is thickest. It is when the lining breaks down, so the line falls, at the start of the cycle.

Your turn. Here are made-up values for lining width. Day 1: 3.0 millimetres. Day 3: 1.5. Day 5: 0.5. Day 14: 2.5. Day 21: 4.0. Day 28: 4.0. Part a: when does menstruation happen? Give a reason. Part b: after day 28, the lining stays thick and no menstruation starts. Suggest why, and name the hormone involved. Pause here and work it out.

Part a. Plot these made-up values. Menstruation is days 1 to 5, because the lining width falls from 3.0 to 0.5 millimetres: the lining is breaking down and leaving the body. Part b. The lining stays at 4.0 millimetres and does not break down, so the egg was probably fertilised. The hormone is progesterone. It stays high and keeps the lining thick, ready for the embryo, so there is no menstruation. So, every time: read the axes and key. Use ruler lines and the right axis. Name the hormone. Link the lining to implantation.
