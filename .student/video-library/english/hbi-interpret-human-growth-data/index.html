Idea. This graph shows made-up example heights. Height increases fastest in infancy, the first year or two of life. Then there is a second fast stage, the growth spurt, in the teenage years. Males usually end up taller than females. Body shape changes too. A newborn's head is about a quarter of its length, and its legs are short. In an adult, the head is only about an eighth of the height, and the legs are about half. These are proportions. A proportion is a part divided by the whole.

Method. Step one: read the headings and units. This made-up table shows height in centimetres for males and females. Step two: give the overall trend first. Height increases with age, from birth to about twenty. Step three: compare the groups and quote numbers. At twenty, males are 177 centimetres and females 163. So males are 14 centimetres taller. From 15 to 20, females grow only 2 centimetres. Step four: to compare people of different sizes, divide the part's length by the total length. For this made-up baby, the head is 12 centimetres, and the length is 48. 12 divided by 48 is 0.25. For an adult, 22 divided by 176 is 0.125. The head did not shrink. It grew more slowly than the body. Babies are checked with a percentile chart. It shows the spread of mass for babies of the same age and sex. Each curve is a percentile. The 50th percentile is the middle: half of babies are lighter than it. On the 9th percentile, only 9 per cent of babies are lighter.

Worked example. Now a real exam question. The diagram shows a percentile chart for baby girls: mass in kilograms against age in weeks, with one curve for each percentile. The question asks you to describe how percentile charts are used to identify concerns about a baby's growth. Three marks. Point one: weigh the baby regularly, for example every few weeks, not just once. Plot each mass against her age on the chart. Point two: compare her points with the percentile curves for girls of the same age. A healthy baby stays close to one curve. Point three: if her points cross the curves, she is gaining or losing too much mass. That is a concern. Here is the full answer. This answer is by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is to say a baby below the 50th percentile is unhealthy. Half of all healthy babies are below it. What matters is a big change over time. Another mistake is to say the head gets smaller as we grow. The head grows. It becomes a smaller proportion of the body. Last, only write conclusions the data shows. Do not write females grow faster unless the numbers show it. And do not give two conclusions that say the same thing.

Your turn, with made-up data. At age 2, a boy's legs are 34 centimetres and his height is 86. At 16, his legs are 82 and his height is 170. Find the leg proportion at each age, and describe the change. Then, a baby girl rises from the 9th percentile at 4 weeks to the 91st at 20 weeks. What does this show? Pause here and work it out.

Answer. Part a. At age 2, 34 divided by 86 is 0.40. At 16, 82 divided by 170 is 0.48. So the legs become a larger proportion of the height. Part b. She has crossed up from the 9th percentile, past the 50th, to the 91st. She is gaining too much mass for her age. This is also a concern. So, every time: read the axes and units. Give the trend first. Compare groups, and quote numbers. For percentile charts, look for a baby crossing curves.
