Read the narration

A spirometer measures the volume of air you breathe. Volume means how much space something takes up. You breathe through a mouthpiece. The air chamber rises when you breathe out, and falls when you breathe in. A pen joined to the chamber draws a trace on a turning drum. Three details matter. A nose clip closes the nose, so all the air goes through the mouthpiece. Soda lime absorbs the carbon dioxide you breathe out. And the mouthpiece is sterilised first, to kill microbes.

Here is a made-up example graph of lung volume. Tidal volume is the volume breathed in or out in one normal breath. On a trace, it is the height from a trough, a low point, up to the next peak, a high point. Vital capacity is the biggest volume you can breathe out after the deepest possible breath in. So it is the full height of the one huge wave. Even after you breathe out as hard as you can, some air stays in your lungs. This is the residual volume, below the lowest point. Vital capacity plus residual volume is the total lung capacity. Step two: to read a volume, read the top and the bottom of the arrow on the volume axis, then subtract. Here, 3.0 minus 2.5 is 0.5. The unit is cubic decimetres. One cubic decimetre is the same as one litre. Step three: after exercise, give two changes. The peaks are higher and the troughs lower, so the breaths are deeper. And the peaks are closer together, so breathing is faster.

Now a real exam question. The trace shows normal breaths, then one very deep breath. It has four arrows: W, X, Y and Z. The question asks you to give the letter and the volume for tidal volume and for vital capacity. Four marks. W is a small arrow on one normal breath, so W is tidal volume. It runs from 2.0 at the trough to 2.4 at the peak. 2.4 minus 2.0 is 0.4 cubic decimetres. Z covers the whole deep breath, from the lowest point to the highest point. So Z is vital capacity. 3.25 minus 1.0 is 2.25 cubic decimetres. X and Y show only part of the deep breath, so they are wrong. Here is the completed table. A common mistake is to call the one huge breath tidal volume. Tidal volume is one normal breath. Also, do not mix up vital capacity with lung capacity, which adds the residual volume. Another mistake is reading only the top of the arrow. Always subtract the bottom. And for exercise, do not just write faster breathing. Say the peaks are closer together, and higher.

Your turn. On Sara's trace, her normal breaths go between 2.7 and 3.3 cubic decimetres. Her deepest breath goes from 1.2 up to 4.7. Give her tidal volume and her vital capacity. Then describe how her trace would change after a fast swim. Pause here and work it out.

For tidal volume, use a normal breath: 3.3 minus 2.7 is 0.6 cubic decimetres. For vital capacity, use the whole deep breath: 4.7 minus 1.2 is 3.5 cubic decimetres. After the swim, her peaks are higher and her troughs lower, because the breaths are deeper. And the peaks are closer together, because she breathes faster. So, every time: match each arrow to the size of breath. Read the top and the bottom, and subtract. For exercise, give depth and rate.
