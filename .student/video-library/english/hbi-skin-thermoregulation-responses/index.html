Think about running on a hot day. Your muscles work hard and respire more, so they release heat. Your face goes red, and your skin gets wet with sweat. Your body is trying to stay at about thirty-seven degrees Celsius. How does the body know? A small part of the brain, the hypothalamus, checks the temperature of the blood. If the blood is too hot or too cold, it sends nerve impulses to the skin and the muscles.

Here is a simple picture of skin. This line is the skin surface. Under it, blood flows round a loop of tiny vessels. The top part is a capillary. The vessel leading up to it is an arteriole. Across the bottom is a short cut, the shunt vessel. When the body is too hot, the arterioles to the capillaries get wider. This is called vasodilation. At the same time, the shunt vessel gets narrower. So more blood flows through the capillaries, near the surface. This warm blood is now close to the air. So more heat is lost from the skin by radiation: heat is given off from the warm skin into the air. This is why hot skin looks red. The capillaries do not move. Only the amount of blood inside them changes. Sweat glands also help. They release sweat onto the skin. Then the water in the sweat evaporates. Evaporation uses heat energy from the body, so the body cools down. When the body is too cold, the opposite happens. The arterioles get narrower: this is vasoconstriction. The shunt vessel gets wider, so less blood flows near the surface, and less heat is lost. Muscles also shiver, which releases heat by respiration.

Now a real exam question. The diagram shows skin on a cold day. On a cold day, most blood goes through the shunt vessel. The question asks you to explain why this skin would be different on a hot day. It is worth four marks. Start from the cold skin. On a cold day, most blood takes the shunt vessel, away from the surface. On a hot day, the body must lose heat. So think: which vessels change? Point one. Vasodilation: the arterioles to the capillaries widen. Point two. The shunt vessel constricts, so it gets narrower. Point three. So more blood is sent through the capillaries, near the skin surface. Point four. More heat is lost from the skin by radiation. Here is the full answer in four points. This answer is written by olevels.com. It is not the official mark scheme.

A very common mistake is to say the capillaries move up to the surface. They do not move. The vessels get wider or narrower, and the amount of blood flowing through them changes. The second mistake is to write only that sweat cools you. You must say that the sweat evaporates, and that evaporation takes heat energy from the body.

Your turn. Omar works in a hot kitchen. He is standing still, but the room is very hot. His face goes red and he sweats a lot. Explain how his skin helps him to lose heat. Pause here and work it out.

The hot air warms Omar's blood. His hypothalamus detects that his blood is warmer, and sends nerve impulses to his skin. Vasodilation sends more blood through the capillaries near the surface, so more heat is lost by radiation. That is why his face is red. He also sweats more, and the sweat evaporates, taking heat from his body. So, every time: decide if the body is too hot or too cold. Start with the hypothalamus. Name the change in the arterioles. Then say how heat is lost: by radiation, and by sweat evaporating.
