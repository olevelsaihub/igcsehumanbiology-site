Read the narration

Air goes down the trachea, the windpipe, then into two bronchi, one for each lung. Air also carries dust and bacteria. Some bacteria are pathogens: microbes that cause disease. So the airways must keep air clean and flowing. The trachea has rings of cartilage. Cartilage is a tough but bendy tissue. Each ring is shaped like a letter C. The rings hold the airway open, so it does not collapse when you breathe in. The gap in the C lets your neck bend.

Step one: name the feature, then say what it does. Start at the nose. Hairs and mucus trap dust and microbes. Mucus is a sticky liquid. The nose also warms the air and makes it moist, which means damp. Air breathed through the mouth is not cleaned or warmed as well. Inside the trachea, the lining has two kinds of cell. Goblet cells make mucus. The sticky mucus traps dust and bacteria. Ciliated cells have cilia: tiny hairs on the top of the cell. The cilia beat together, like oars. They sweep the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed. So dust and bacteria are carried away from the lungs. Step two: always add the result. Think feature, job, result. Nose hairs and mucus trap dust, so cleaner air reaches the lungs. Mucus traps pathogens and cilia remove them, so there are fewer infections. Cartilage holds the airway open, so air flows freely. Step three: for damage, follow the chain. Tar is a sticky chemical in cigarette smoke. It can paralyse the cilia, so they stop beating, or destroy them. Then the mucus builds up. It narrows or blocks the airways. Bacteria stay in the lungs, so the person coughs and gets more infections. This is chronic bronchitis: a long-lasting disease where mucus blocks the airways. A narrow airway lets less air through each second. So each breath must last longer to move the same volume of air.

Now a real exam question. The diagram shows two airways, cut across. The one on the right has chronic bronchitis, with excess mucus and damaged cilia. Excess means too much. The question asks you to explain how these affect the breathing system. Two marks. First, link the two features. The damaged cilia cannot sweep the mucus away. So the mucus builds up and blocks the airways. That is point one. Next, think about what is in the mucus: trapped bacteria. They are not removed, so they stay in the lungs. That means a higher risk of lung infections, and a lot of coughing. That is point two. Here is the full answer: what happens, then the result. A common mistake is to say the cilia trap dust. They do not. The mucus traps dust and bacteria. The cilia only move the mucus. Cilia do not filter the air or kill bacteria either. Another mistake is stopping too early. Cartilage does not just make the trachea strong: it stops it collapsing, so air flows freely. And after more mucus, give the result: blocked airways or more infections.

Your turn. A baby is born with soft, weak rings of cartilage in her trachea. Her trachea can partly collapse when she breathes in. Explain the normal job of the rings. Then explain how weak rings affect the air reaching her lungs. Pause here and work it out.

Normally, the C-shaped rings hold the trachea open, so it does not collapse, and air flows freely. Her rings are weak, so her trachea narrows when she breathes in. Less air passes through each second, so less air reaches her lungs. So, every time: name the feature, say its job, then give the result. For damage, follow the chain: cilia stop, mucus builds up, airways block, infection.
