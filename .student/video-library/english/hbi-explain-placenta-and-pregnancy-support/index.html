Explain how the placenta, cord and hormones support a fetus

Read the narration

Idea. A fetus is a baby growing in the uterus, or womb. It cannot eat or breathe. It gets everything from its mother. The placenta is an organ on the uterus wall. The umbilical cord joins the fetus to the placenta. Amniotic fluid around the fetus protects it from bumps. In the placenta, the mother's blood and the fetus's blood come very close, but they never mix. Substances diffuse across, from high to low concentration. Oxygen, glucose and amino acids pass to the fetus. Carbon dioxide and urea, a waste made from extra amino acids, pass back to the mother.

Method. Step one: name the substance and its route. Oxygen and glucose go from the mother's blood, across the placenta, and along the cord to the fetus. Waste goes the other way. Step two: say what it is used for. Oxygen and glucose are used in aerobic respiration, the reaction in cells that releases energy from glucose using oxygen. That energy is used for growth. Amino acids are used to make proteins for new cells. Step three: give the effect. If the fetus gets less glucose and oxygen, there is less respiration, so less energy. That means less growth and a lower birth mass. Harmful substances also cross the placenta, such as nicotine, alcohol and some drugs. They can damage the growing fetus and its development. Some can also pass into breast milk. Hormones keep the pregnancy going. A hormone is a chemical carried in the blood. The embryo, the early stage of the baby, makes a hormone called hCG. It keeps the corpus luteum alive. This is what is left of the follicle in the ovary after the egg is released. It makes progesterone, which keeps the uterus lining thick. Later, the corpus luteum breaks down, and the placenta makes the progesterone.

Worked example. Now a real exam question. The diagram shows identical twins that share one placenta. The question asks you to explain why twins sharing a placenta have a lower mass when they are born. Three marks. Point one: the placenta passes glucose and oxygen from the mother's blood to the fetus. Point two: here, two twins share one placenta, so each gets less. Point three: so each fetus carries out less aerobic respiration. Point four: less energy is released, so less growth happens, and they are born lighter. Here is the full answer. Common mistake. A common mistake is to say the mother's blood mixes with the baby's blood. It never mixes. Substances diffuse across the thin barrier. Another mistake is to write: the twins get less food. Name glucose and oxygen, and link them to respiration and energy. And never say the placenta makes energy. Last, the corpus luteum does not keep the lining thick by itself. It makes progesterone, and the progesterone keeps the lining.

Your turn. High up in the mountains, the air has less oxygen. A pregnant woman living there has less oxygen in her blood. Suggest why her baby may have a lower birth mass. Then explain why the placenta must make progesterone late in pregnancy. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. Part a. Her blood has less oxygen, so less oxygen diffuses across the placenta to the fetus. The fetus carries out less aerobic respiration. Less energy is released, so there is less growth and a lower birth mass. Part b. Late in pregnancy, the corpus luteum has broken down. So the placenta must make the progesterone. Progesterone keeps the lining thick, so the pregnancy continues. So, every time: name the substance and its route. Say what it is used for. Then give the effect on the fetus. For hormones, follow the chain to the lining.
