Prepare cheek-cell or blood slides for a light microscope

Read the narration

Human cells are thin, and almost see-through. Under a light microscope, light must pass through them. With no stain, you can hardly see them. A stain is a dye that colours parts of the cell, so they show up clearly.

Step one: collect the cells cleanly. Gently rub a sterile cotton bud on the inside of your cheek. Sterile means it has no microbes on it. Microbes are tiny living things, such as bacteria. Step two: smear the cells thinly on a clean slide. A thin layer lets light pass through one layer of cells. Step three: add a drop of stain, such as methylene blue. Step four: lower a coverslip at an angle, so no air bubbles are trapped. A coverslip is a thin square of glass. Then soak up extra stain with a paper towel. Step five: put the slide on the stage. Start with the low power objective lens, then focus. For blood, prick a finger with a sterile lancet, a small sharp blade. Then let the smear dry before you stain it.

The most common mistake is to forget the stain. Then the cells are almost see-through, and the student sees nothing. Other mistakes: a thick blob instead of a thin smear, a cotton bud that is not sterile, or putting the slide on the stage before the cells are on it.

Your turn. A student wants to look at white blood cells. First, describe how to obtain the blood and prepare the slide. Second, another student saw no cells at all. Suggest why. Pause here and work it out.

Part a. Prick a finger with a sterile lancet. Smear the blood thinly on a clean slide, and let it dry. Add a stain, such as methylene blue. Lower a coverslip at an angle. Part b. The student probably did not add a stain. The cells are almost see-through, so they could not be seen. So, every time: collect with something sterile, smear thinly, stain, then add a coverslip at an angle. The slide goes on the stage last.
