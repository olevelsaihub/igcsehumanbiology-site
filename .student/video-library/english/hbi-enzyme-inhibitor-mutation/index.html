Explain how inhibitors or a mutation change enzyme activity

Idea. Enzymes are biological catalysts. A catalyst speeds up a reaction without being used up. Each enzyme is a protein with an active site, a pocket with a special shape. Only a substrate with a matching shape fits. The substrate is the molecule the enzyme acts on. Together they form an enzyme-substrate complex. Then the products are made and leave. The active site is free again for the next substrate.

Method. Step one: identify the cause. A competitive inhibitor has a shape like the substrate. It enters the active site and blocks it. So the substrate cannot bind, and fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form. If you add more substrate, it has a better chance of reaching a free active site. So with a competitive inhibitor, the rate can still reach the normal maximum. A non-competitive inhibitor binds to another part of the enzyme. This changes the shape of the active site, so the substrate cannot fit. Adding more substrate does not help. A mutation is a change in a gene. It can change the order of amino acids in the enzyme. Then the active site has a different shape. The substrate may not fit, so the enzyme works slowly, or not at all. On this made-up graph, the line with no inhibitor rises fastest. With a competitive inhibitor, it rises more slowly, but nearly reaches the same maximum. With a non-competitive inhibitor, it levels off at a lower maximum. Some drugs are inhibitors. Aspirin inhibits an enzyme in the clotting pathway. So the chemicals that make platelets stick together are not released, and fewer clots form. Step four: always finish with the effect.

Worked example. Now a real exam question. An enzyme makes a gas. A student collects the gas every ten minutes for an hour. The bar chart shows the results. Explain the effect on the results if a competitive inhibitor were added at the start. Two marks. Here are the student's results. Each bar is the gas made in ten minutes. Now add a competitive inhibitor. It has a shape like the substrate, so it enters the active sites and blocks them. So fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form. The reaction is slower, so less gas is made. The bars would be lower. Here is the full answer. It is by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is to say a competitive inhibitor changes the shape of the active site. It does not. It only blocks it. A non-competitive inhibitor changes the shape. Another mistake is to say the inhibitor kills the enzyme. Enzymes are not alive. And always finish with the effect, such as less gas.

Your turn. Lactase is the enzyme that digests lactose, the sugar in milk. A person has a mutation in the gene for lactase. Explain why this person may not digest lactose well. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. The mutation changes the order of amino acids in lactase. So its active site has a different shape. Lactose no longer fits. So fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form, and less lactose is broken down. So, every time: identify the cause. Say what happens to the active site: blocked, or a new shape. Say fewer complexes form. Then give the effect in the question's own words.
