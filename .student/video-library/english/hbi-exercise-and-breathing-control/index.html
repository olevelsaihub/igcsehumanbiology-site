Explain heart and breathing changes in exercise and how carbon dioxide controls breathing

Idea. During exercise, your muscles contract more. So they need more energy. Your breathing rate and your heart rate both go up. After you stop, they stay high for a while, then slowly fall back. Why? Energy is released by respiration. Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and glucose, and it makes carbon dioxide. So working muscles need more oxygen and glucose, and they make more carbon dioxide.

Method. The heart beats faster, so more blood flows to the muscles each minute. The blood brings more oxygen and glucose, and takes away more carbon dioxide. So more aerobic respiration can happen. Blood is also sent to where it is needed. Less blood goes to the gut, liver and kidneys. More goes to the working muscles. What controls breathing? Faster respiration makes more carbon dioxide, so the blood becomes more acidic. Chemoreceptors in the aorta and the carotid arteries detect this. A chemoreceptor is a cell that detects a chemical. They send nerve impulses, electrical messages, to the medulla oblongata, in the brain. The medulla then sends impulses to the breathing muscles and the heart. Breathing gets faster and deeper, and the heart beats faster. This removes the extra carbon dioxide. Two more controls. Adrenaline is a hormone, a chemical messenger carried in the blood. Adrenaline raises heart rate, widens the airways, and makes the liver release glucose. And the vagus nerve normally slows the heart. If it is blocked, the heart rate rises. After exercise, the rates stay high. During hard exercise, muscles also respire without oxygen, which makes lactic acid. Extra oxygen, called the oxygen debt, is needed to break the lactic acid down.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. A student exercised and measured their pulse rate. The pulse rose from about seventy to about one hundred and forty beats per minute over one hundred seconds. The question asks: explain the change in pulse rate. It is worth four marks. Point one: describe it. As the time spent exercising increases, the pulse rate increases, from about seventy to about one hundred and forty beats per minute. Point two: the heart beats faster, so more blood flows to the muscles. Point three: this brings more oxygen and glucose, for more aerobic respiration. Point four: it removes more carbon dioxide, and less lactic acid builds up. Here is the full answer. This explanation is by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is to say the muscles make energy. Energy is released by respiration. Write: more aerobic respiration releases more energy. Another mistake is to say oxygen levels control breathing. In your answer, use carbon dioxide. And say where it is detected: chemoreceptors in the aorta and the carotid arteries.

Your turn. A boy breathes in and out of a paper bag, at rest. The air in the bag gets more and more carbon dioxide. Explain why his breathing becomes faster and deeper. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. He breathes in more carbon dioxide, so more carbon dioxide gets into his blood. Chemoreceptors in his aorta and carotid arteries detect it. They send impulses to the medulla. The medulla sends impulses to his breathing muscles, so breathing gets faster and deeper, to remove the carbon dioxide. Every time: describe the change with numbers. Link it to the muscles' needs: oxygen, glucose and respiration. For control, give the detector, then the medulla, then the effect.
