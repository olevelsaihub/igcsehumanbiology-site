Read the narration

Bone is living tissue. Minerals are built into it. The main ones are calcium and phosphate. Together, they make bone hard and strong. So to grow bone, and to keep it strong, your diet must supply these. Without enough calcium, bones can lose mass. In osteoporosis, bone becomes less dense, with larger spaces inside. Dense means a lot of mass packed into a space. So the bones are brittle, which means they break easily.

Step one: name each nutrient, then give its job. Calcium is absorbed from food in the small intestine, into the blood. Vitamin D helps the small intestine absorb the calcium. The blood carries it to the bones, where it is laid down. Protein is needed to build muscle, and for bone to grow. Milk, cheese and yoghurt contain calcium, protein and some vitamin D. So link each nutrient to its job, and then to stronger bone or muscle. Step three: for osteoporosis, give the change, then the effects. The bone is less dense, so it fractures, or breaks, easily. The person may have back pain, a stooped, bent posture, and they lose height. Causes of osteoporosis: old age; too little calcium or vitamin D in the diet; and too little weight-bearing exercise, such as walking, where your legs carry your weight. Also low oestrogen, a female hormone, after the menopause, when periods stop.

Now a real exam question. The table gives the nutrients in a pot of yoghurt. Part d asks: explain why yoghurt is good for bone development. Five marks, so plan five separate points. First, read the table. The yoghurt contains calcium, vitamin D and protein. All three help bones, so name each one. Vitamin D helps the small intestine absorb calcium into the blood. The calcium is laid down in bone, forming hard compact bone. Compact bone is the dense outer layer of bone. So the bones are stronger and denser, and less likely to break. For a spare mark, add protein: it is needed for bones to grow and develop. Five marks, so give more than five ideas. Here is the full answer. A common mistake is to say vitamin D makes bones. It does not. It helps calcium get absorbed. Another is naming calcium without saying what it does. Also, do not say osteoporosis makes bones soft. Say the bone is less dense, with larger spaces, so it is brittle and breaks easily. And remember protein for muscle.

Your turn. A 70-year-old woman, long past the menopause, rarely walks anywhere. She breaks her wrist in a small fall. Her doctor says she has osteoporosis. Describe how her bone has changed. Give two likely causes. Then explain why the doctor gives her vitamin D tablets. Pause here and work it out.

Her bone is less dense, with larger spaces, so it is brittle and broke easily. Likely causes: her age, low oestrogen after the menopause, and too little weight-bearing exercise, because she rarely walks. For part c: vitamin D helps her small intestine absorb calcium. More calcium is laid down in her bones, so they become denser and stronger, and less likely to break. So, every time: name the nutrient, give its job, then link it to strong bone or muscle.
