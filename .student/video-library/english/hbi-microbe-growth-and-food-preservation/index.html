Read the narration

Bacteria in food grow in four phases. In this made-up example graph: first the lag phase, when they are making enzymes, not dividing yet. Then the exponential phase: numbers double again and again. Then the stationary phase: food runs short and waste builds up, so numbers stay level. Last, the death phase: numbers fall. Bacteria multiply fastest when they have three things: food, moisture, which means water, and warmth near their optimum temperature. The optimum is the temperature where their enzymes work best. Food preservation takes away one of these.

Step one: name the condition, such as temperature, moisture, food, or air. Step two: say what it does to the bacteria's enzymes, or to how fast they divide. Step three: state the result, so the food spoils faster or slower. Most bacteria in food grow fastest near body temperature, 37 degrees Celsius. Cold slows bacteria. A fridge, at about 4 degrees Celsius, slows their enzymes, so they multiply slowly. It does not kill them. Freezing stops growth for longer, but bacteria grow again after thawing. Heat kills. Boiling, pasteurising, which means heating milk to about 72 degrees Celsius for a short time, and thorough cooking denature the bacteria's enzymes: their shape changes, so they stop working. Most bacteria die. Sealing or vacuum packing keeps air and new microbes out. Step four, for a graph: different groups of bacteria have different optimum temperatures. In this made-up example, psychrophiles like cold. Mesophiles grow best near body temperature. Thermophiles grow best at about 50 to 70 degrees Celsius. Read the named group's optimum, and compare.

Now a real exam question. Bacteria multiply rapidly in food that is not preserved. Use words from the box to complete the passage. There are six blanks and six marks. The passage follows the curve. Blank one: the first phase, when bacteria are not dividing. That is lag. Blank two: bacteria multiplying rapidly. That is exponential. Blank three: nutrients run out, and numbers stay level. So dying cells are equal to dividing cells. Blank four: the last phase is death, the word in the box. In the death phase, numbers fall. So dividing cells are less than the number of dying cells. Here is the full answer. This answer is by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme. Notice that stationary is not used: the third stage is described, not named.

A common mistake is to say a fridge kills bacteria. Cold only slows them down. Another is to call the not-dividing phase stationary. That is the lag phase. Also, do not put more than in the death phase. Dividing cells are fewer than dying cells. And do not assume every bacterium dies at 65 degrees Celsius: thermophiles grow well there. Freezing is not heat treatment.

Your turn. On a camping trip, cooked rice is kept either in a hot car at 35 degrees Celsius, or in a cool box at 5 degrees Celsius. Explain why the rice in the car spoils faster. A friend says the cool box kills the bacteria: explain why they are wrong. Then explain why dried fruit keeps for months. Pause here and work it out.

Part a: 35 degrees Celsius is close to the optimum temperature of most bacteria in food, so their enzymes work fast and they multiply quickly. Part b: at 5 degrees Celsius, their enzymes are only slowed. The bacteria are still alive and grow again when the rice warms up. Part c: drying removes water. Bacteria need moisture to grow, so in dried fruit they cannot multiply, and the fruit keeps for months. So, every time: name the condition, say what it does to the enzymes or to division, then state the result. For a word box, check that the comparison is true for that phase.
