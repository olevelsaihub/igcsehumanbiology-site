Idea. A food test shows if a nutrient is in a food. You add a reagent, and look for a colour change. A reagent is a chemical that reacts with the nutrient. For example, biuret turns from blue to lilac when protein is there. If the colour does not change, the result is negative. The nutrient is not there. A positive result means the nutrient is present.

Method. Step one: prepare the sample. Grind or chop solid food, mix it with water, and put some in a test tube. Wear eye protection. Step two: add the right reagent and do the key action. A reducing sugar is a simple sugar, such as glucose. To test for it, add Benedict's solution and heat it in a water bath. Blue turns brick red. Use a water bath, not a naked flame. For starch, add iodine solution. Orange-brown turns blue-black. For protein, add biuret. Blue turns lilac. Do not heat these two. For lipid, shake the food with ethanol. Then pour it into water. A milky white emulsion means lipid. An emulsion is tiny drops of fat spread through water. For vitamin C, use blue DCPIP. Add the juice drop by drop to a fixed volume of DCPIP, until the blue colour goes. Count the drops. Fewer drops means more vitamin C. To compare two juices, keep the volume of DCPIP the same, and repeat. Some questions do it the other way round: add DCPIP drop by drop to a fixed volume of juice. Then the more DCPIP that loses its colour, the more vitamin C.

Worked example. Now a real exam question. It comes from a question about a capillary network. Plasma is the liquid part of blood. Part b asks: describe how a sample of plasma could be tested for proteins. Four marks. Mark one: the sample. Put about two cubic centimetres of plasma into a clean test tube. Mark two: the reagent. Add biuret solution and shake gently. Do not heat it. Marks three and four: the result and its meaning. If the blue turns lilac, protein is present. If it stays blue, there is no protein. Here is the full answer. It is by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is heating the wrong test. Only Benedict's needs heating. Biuret and iodine are never heated. Another mistake is the order in the emulsion test. Shake the food with ethanol first. Then add the water.

Your turn. Describe how you could test some fruit juice for reducing sugar, safely. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. Put some juice in a test tube. Add an equal volume of Benedict's solution. Heat it in a water bath, and wear eye protection. If the blue turns brick red, reducing sugar is present. If it stays blue, there is none. So, every time: prepare the sample. Add the right reagent. Do the key action. Then give the colour change, and what it means.
