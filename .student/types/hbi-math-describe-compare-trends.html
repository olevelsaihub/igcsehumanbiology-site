Describe a trend or compare data sets and conclude

Describing the pattern in one or more data series from a graph, table or chart, comparing groups or conditions, and drawing a conclusion the data support.

Type 15 Maths skills HBI-MATH-DESCRIBE-COMPARE-TRENDS

★★★★★ · 21 of 36 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Medium.

Students must spot changes in shape, compare groups and decide what the data can support. Relationships must be stated with both variables; conclusions must not go beyond the data. Reading values and sometimes subtracting to quote the size of a change. Data come as line graphs, bar charts, frequency charts and tables in many contexts. Some items link to topic knowledge for an explanation or a conclusion.

Knowledge card

Describe a trend or compare data sets and conclude

Written
  • A trend is the overall direction of the data: it increases, decreases, rises then falls, or stays the same. Always say the direction.
  • Link both variables in one sentence, the right way round: 'as light intensity increases, the pupil diameter decreases'.
  • Point out where the shape changes and give the value there: a peak (optimum), a lowest point, where it levels off, where it gets steeper or slows down. Say when values are highest and lowest.
  • Quote data with units to support each point: start and end values, the turning point, or the size of the change (for example 'falls from 82% to 72%, a fall of 10%').
  • Real data often go up and down from one year to the next. Say that the data fluctuate, as well as giving the overall trend.
  • When you compare groups, use comparing words: do both groups show the same trend over the whole range; which group is higher all the time; which changes more or faster; which items rise and which fall; where the gap is biggest.
  • For two curves over time (blood glucose with and without diabetes, antibodies after each dose), compare the starting level, how high it rises, how fast it rises, the gap between highest and lowest, and how quickly it goes back down.
  • For a frequency bar chart, say where most values are (the middle groups) and where there are few (the two ends), and where the highest bar is.
  • A conclusion must come from the data: use 'suggests' or 'appears to', and give the evidence. If one variable rises steadily while the other goes up and down, the data suggest the two are not linked.
  • Watch for special features: a delay (lag) before an effect appears, an inverse relationship (one rises as the other falls), and gaps between values that get bigger each step.

Before you begin

  • Reading graphs, bar charts and tables, including graphs with two y-axes or a key.
  • Words for change: increase, decrease, peak, level off, fluctuate, steeper.
  • Subtracting two readings to quote the size of a change.
  • Topic knowledge for explain items (enzymes, blood clotting, disease).
1

The method

  1. Read the axes, the key and the command word. Decide if you must describe, compare, conclude or explain.

    Why: Each command word is marked differently; an explanation earns nothing in a describe question.

  2. State the overall trend, using both variables, for each line or group.

    Why: The first mark is nearly always the overall direction.

  3. Find each change in shape (peak, lowest point, levelling off, steeper part, fluctuation) and say where it happens.

    Why: Further marks are for changes in shape at stated values or years.

  4. Quote data with units: start, turning point, end, or the size of the change.

    Why: Numbers support the description, and some schemes give a mark for a quoted change.

  5. For two or more groups, use comparing words: same trend or not, higher throughout, changes more or faster, biggest gap.

    Why: Compare marks need a direct comparison, not two separate descriptions.

  6. For a conclusion, write what the data suggest and give the evidence. Do not claim more than the data show.

    Why: Schemes credit supported conclusions, such as one group always being higher.

  7. Only for 'explain', add the biological reason after each feature you describe.

    Why: Explain marks need the cause, for example aspirin breaking down the clot faster.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Writing 'it changes' without saying up or down, or swapping the variables ('as time to digest increases, pH increases').
  • ✗
    Describing only the start and the end, and missing a peak, a steeper section or the fluctuations.
  • ✗
    Describing each group separately ('males go up, females go up') without saying which is higher or which changes more.
  • ✗
    Listing every value instead of describing the pattern.
  • ✗
    Saying the data 'prove' a cause, or explaining causes when only a description is asked for.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Give7Give the asked number of separate trends, differences or conclusions, one per line, each a different idea backed by the data.

Worked explanation

  1. The graph shows time taken to digest starch against pH. The values are pH 2: 60 min, pH 4: 45 min, pH 6: 20 min, pH 8: 35 min, pH 10: 55 min.
  2. From pH 2 to pH 6 the time goes down.
  3. The shortest time is 20 minutes at pH 6. This is the turning point.
  4. From pH 6 to pH 10 the time goes up again, to 55 minutes.
  5. So I describe two parts and name pH 6.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

As the pH rises from 2 to 6, the time taken to digest starch falls, from 60 minutes to a lowest value of 20 minutes at pH 6. Above pH 6 the time taken rises again as the pH increases, reaching 55 minutes at pH 10.

How the marks are earned

One mark for saying the time falls as the pH rises up to pH 6. One mark for saying the time rises again from pH 6 to pH 10. If the turning point is not named, only one mark is given for 'decreases then increases'.
