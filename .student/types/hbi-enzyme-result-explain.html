Explain an enzyme practical result, including levelling off as substrate runs out

Observations such as falling pH, a positive sugar test, a clearing film or a plateau in product, explained by digestion and by substrate becoming limiting.

Type 10 Nutrition and energy HBI-ENZYME-RESULT-EXPLAIN

★★★★☆ · 7 of 36 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Medium.

Two or three linked steps are needed, from the enzyme to the visible result. Uses standard enzyme, substrate and product pairs. No calculation; tables are read only for trends. Each paper uses a different practical (film, Visking tubing, indicator, gas), so the set-up must be understood first. Combines digestion knowledge with reading practical data.

Knowledge card

Explain an enzyme practical result, including levelling off as substrate runs out

Written
  • Amylase digests starch into maltose (a sugar); maltase then turns maltose into glucose.
  • Lipase digests lipids (fats and oils) into fatty acids and glycerol. Fatty acids are acids, so the pH falls as they are made.
  • Proteases such as pepsin digest protein into amino acids (via peptides). These products are small and soluble, so a protein layer dissolves away.
  • Starch is a large molecule and cannot pass through Visking tubing; sugar is small enough to diffuse through it.
  • A reaction slows and stops when the substrate is used up. The amount of product then stays the same.
  • If adding more enzyme no longer speeds up the reaction, the substrate is now the limiting factor: there is not enough substrate to fill the extra active sites.
  • At the start there is plenty of substrate, so many enzyme-substrate complexes form and the rate is high. As the substrate is used up, fewer complexes form, so less product (for example less gas) is made in each time interval.
  • Saliva contains amylase, a carbohydrase. Kept at 37 °C with starch, it makes sugar, so the Benedict's test turns brick red.
  • On photographic film, pepsin digests the protein layer into soluble amino acids or peptides. The black silver compounds held in the protein are freed and fall away, so the film becomes clear.
  • If an acid product (such as lactic acid or fatty acids) builds up, the pH can fall so low that the enzyme denatures. This is a second reason a reaction can stop.

Before you begin

  • Enzymes are biological catalysts with an active site that fits one substrate.
  • Food tests: iodine (starch), Benedict's (reducing sugar), biuret (protein).
  • Low pH means more acidic.
  • Diffusion of small molecules through a partially permeable membrane.
1

The method

  1. Name the enzyme, even if the question does not.

    Why: Many schemes give a separate mark for naming lipase, amylase or pepsin.

  2. Say what substrate it digests.

    Why: The breakdown of the substrate is a marking point in almost every scheme.

  3. Name the product exactly (maltose or glucose, fatty acids, amino acids).

    Why: The product is a separate mark; a vague word like 'smaller pieces' may not score.

  4. Link the product to the observation: acid lowers pH, sugar turns Benedict's brick red, soluble products wash away, small sugar diffuses out.

    Why: The final mark is for connecting the chemistry to what was actually seen.

  5. For a plateau, say the substrate is used up or is now limiting, so the rate falls or stops.

    Why: Levelling-off marks need the cause (substrate) and the effect (rate slows or product stays constant).

  6. For a bar chart or table over time, explain the start (lots of substrate, fast) and the end (little substrate left, slow) separately.

    Why: Schemes give separate marks for the early high rate and the later fall.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Writing 'the enzyme makes the pH go down' without naming fatty acids as the acid that is made.
  • ✗
    Saying lipase turns fat into glucose, or amylase turns starch into amino acids.
  • ✗
    Explaining a plateau by saying 'the enzyme is used up' instead of the substrate being used up.
  • ✗
    Saying starch moved out through the Visking tubing, when only the small sugar can diffuse out.
  • ✗
    Describing the numbers in the table again instead of explaining them.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. The question asks why the pH falls over 25 hours. It is worth 3 marks, so I need three linked ideas.
  2. Which enzyme is in the tube? Lipase. What does it act on? Olive oil, which is a lipid.
  3. What does lipase make? Fatty acids and glycerol.
  4. Why does that change the pH? Fatty acids are acids, so as more are made the mixture becomes more acidic and the pH goes down.
  5. Check: enzyme and substrate (1), product (1), link to pH (1). Three marks covered.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Lipase breaks down the olive oil (a lipid) into fatty acids and glycerol. The fatty acids are acidic, so as more of them are made the solution becomes more acidic and its pH falls.

How the marks are earned

One mark for saying lipase digests the oil, one for naming fatty acids as a product, and one for saying that these acids make the pH go down. Glycerol is not needed for a mark, but it does no harm.
