Explain how temperature or pH changes enzyme activity

Explaining rising, falling or zero enzyme activity as temperature or pH changes, why tests are kept at 37 °C or one pH, and why body temperature and pH must stay near normal.

Type 7 Biological molecules HBI-ENZYME-TEMP-PH-DENATURE

★★★★★ · 12 of 36 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Medium.

A four- or five-step causal chain, plus deciding which side of the optimum applies. Kinetic energy, collisions and denaturing by heat or pH at the molecular level. Graph and table reading only, no calculation. Contexts vary (hypothermia, biosensors, yoghurt, catalase, pepsin) but the same two chains apply. Links enzymes to homeostasis, digestion and practical work.

Knowledge card

Explain how temperature or pH changes enzyme activity

Written
  • Enzymes are biological catalysts: proteins that speed up reactions in the body without being used up. Each has an active site with a specific shape.
  • Below the optimum temperature, warming gives enzyme and substrate molecules more kinetic energy. They collide more often, so more enzyme-substrate complexes form and the rate rises.
  • At low temperature the molecules have less kinetic energy, so there are fewer collisions and fewer complexes. The rate is slow, but the enzyme is not damaged.
  • Above the optimum temperature the enzyme is denatured: the active site changes shape, the substrate no longer fits, and few or no enzyme-substrate complexes form.
  • Each enzyme also has an optimum pH. Too acidic or too alkaline a pH changes the shape of the active site (denatures it), so the substrate cannot bind and activity falls or stops.
  • The optimum pH matches where the enzyme works: pepsin in the stomach works best at about pH 1 to 2; lipase works best in slightly alkaline conditions, so sodium hydrogencarbonate is added in experiments to give it its best pH.
  • Gut enzyme tests are done at 37 °C because it is body temperature and near the optimum, so the enzyme works fast and is not denatured.
  • If body temperature falls (hypothermia), enzymes lose kinetic energy, metabolism slows, and the heart rate and breathing slow, which can kill. If it rises too high, enzymes are denatured and metabolism falls.
  • In a biosensor or any comparison, keep the temperature the same: a warmer sample makes the enzyme work faster, so more product forms and the reading is higher.
  • Bacteria that make acid, such as those in yoghurt, stop working when the pH becomes too low, because the acid denatures their enzymes.

Before you begin

  • Enzymes are proteins with a specific active site.
  • Particles move faster when heated (kinetic energy).
  • The pH scale: acid below 7, alkaline above 7.
  • Body temperature is about 37 °C and is controlled by homeostasis.
1

The method

  1. Name the factor (temperature or pH) and decide which side of the optimum the change is on.

    Why: Below the optimum and above it (or at the wrong pH) need different explanations.

  2. Rising temperature below the optimum: more kinetic energy, more collisions, more enzyme-substrate complexes, faster rate.

    Why: Each link in this chain is a separate mark.

  3. Too hot, or wrong pH: enzyme denatured, active site changes shape, substrate cannot bind, fewer complexes, rate falls.

    Why: Each of these ideas is credited separately.

  4. For pH data, say which pH is at or near the optimum and which is not, then explain the one that is not.

    Why: Schemes usually give a mark for comparing with the optimum before the denaturing chain.

  5. For 'why keep it at 37 °C' or 'at the same temperature', say what temperature does to the rate and so to the result (time, reading, amount of product).

    Why: Control-variable questions want the effect on the measurement, not only the biology.

  6. For body questions, end with the effect on metabolism and one symptom, such as a slow heart rate or breathing rate.

    Why: Context questions keep the last mark for the effect on the person.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Saying the enzyme is 'killed'; enzymes are not alive, they are denatured.
  • ✗
    Saying low temperature denatures the enzyme.
  • ✗
    Saying the substrate changes shape instead of the active site.
  • ✗
    Saying every enzyme has an optimum of pH 7 and 37 °C (pepsin works best at about pH 2).
  • ✗
    Writing 'the enzyme works faster' with no mention of collisions or enzyme-substrate complexes.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Look at the graph: A is at about 43 °C and B at about 58 °C. Activity rises from A to B. So this is the rising side, before the optimum.
  2. The cause is the rise in temperature. I say that first.
  3. Higher temperature gives the enzyme and substrate molecules more kinetic energy, so they move faster.
  4. They collide more often, so more enzyme-substrate complexes form each second.
  5. So the rate of enzyme activity increases. That is five linked ideas for five marks. I will not mention denaturing, because that happens after B.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

From A to B the temperature increases. The enzyme and substrate molecules gain more kinetic energy and move faster. This means they collide more often, so more enzyme-substrate complexes are formed every second. As a result, more product is made per second and the rate of enzyme activity rises.

How the marks are earned

One mark each for: the rise in temperature; more kinetic energy; more collisions; more enzyme-substrate complexes; a higher rate of activity. Five ideas for five marks, so every link must be written.
