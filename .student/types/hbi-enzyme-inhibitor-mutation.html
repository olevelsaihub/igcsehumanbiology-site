Knowledge card

Explain how inhibitors or a mutation change enzyme activity

Written
  • Each enzyme has an active site with a specific shape. Only a substrate with a complementary shape fits it and forms an enzyme-substrate complex.
  • A competitive inhibitor has a shape similar to the substrate. It enters the active site and blocks it, so fewer substrate molecules can bind.
  • With a competitive inhibitor, adding more substrate gives the substrate a better chance of reaching the active sites, so the rate rises more slowly but can reach the normal maximum.
  • A non-competitive inhibitor binds to another part of the enzyme and changes the shape of the active site. Adding more substrate does not help, so the maximum rate is lower.
  • With any inhibitor, fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form, so less product is made in the same time.
  • Some drugs are inhibitors. Aspirin inhibits an enzyme in the clotting pathway, so the chemicals that make platelets stick together are not released and fewer clots form.
  • A mutation can change the amino acid order of an enzyme, so its active site has a different shape. The substrate may no longer fit, so the enzyme works slowly or not at all.
  • On a graph of rate against substrate concentration, the curve without inhibitor rises more steeply and levels off sooner; a non-competitive inhibitor gives a curve that levels off at a lower maximum.

Before you begin

  • Enzymes are proteins made of amino acids, with an active site.
  • Lock and key idea: the substrate fits the active site.
  • A mutation changes the base sequence and can change the amino acid sequence.
1

The method

  1. Identify the cause: competitive inhibitor, non-competitive inhibitor or mutation.

    Why: Each cause acts on the active site in a different way.

  2. Say what happens to the active site: blocked (competitive) or changed in shape (non-competitive or mutation).

    Why: The active site idea is credited in every scheme for this type.

  3. Say the substrate cannot bind, so fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form.

    Why: This links the active site to the rate.

  4. Finish with the effect in the question's own terms: less gas, a slower rate, less product, or fewer clots.

    Why: The effect on the results is usually a separate mark.

  5. In a pathway diagram (such as clotting), follow the arrows after the inhibited enzyme to the final effect.

    Why: Later marks are for the steps that no longer happen, such as platelets not sticking together.

  6. For sketches: without the competitive inhibitor, draw a similar curve that rises faster and levels off sooner; for a non-competitive inhibitor, say the same maximum is never reached even with more substrate.

    Why: Curve questions credit the shape and where the curve levels off.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Saying a competitive inhibitor changes the shape of the active site.
  • ✗
    Saying the substrate changes shape instead of the active site.
  • ✗
    Saying the inhibitor 'kills' or 'destroys' the enzyme.
  • ✗
    Saying that adding more substrate overcomes a non-competitive inhibitor.
  • ✗
    Stopping at 'the enzyme is blocked' without saying what happens to the product, such as less gas or fewer clots.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. The enzyme makes a gas, and the gas is collected every 10 minutes for one hour. The question asks what a competitive inhibitor added at the start would do to the results.
  2. A competitive inhibitor has a shape like the substrate, so it fits into the active sites of the enzyme and blocks them.
  3. Substrate cannot enter a blocked active site, so fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form.
  4. So the reaction is slower and less gas is collected in each 10-minute period: every bar on the chart would be lower.
  5. There are 2 marks: one for the effect on the results and one for the reason. I write both.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Less gas would be produced in each 10-minute period, so every bar would be lower, because the reaction is slower. This is because the competitive inhibitor enters and blocks the active sites of the enzyme, so fewer substrate molecules can bind and fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form.

How the marks are earned

Mark 1: less gas is produced, or the reaction is slower. Mark 2: the reason, that the inhibitor blocks the active sites. An answer that gives the effect with no active-site reason earns only one mark.
