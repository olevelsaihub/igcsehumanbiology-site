Plan an investigation measuring a response or treatment in people

Pulse, reaction time, pupil size, catching, hearing, sweating or urine volume in volunteers, and treated versus untreated group trials of drugs or diets.

Knowledge card

Plan an investigation measuring a response or treatment in people

Written
  • Pulse rate: place two fingers on the wrist or neck and count beats for 15 seconds, then multiply by 4 (or use a pulse monitor).
  • Always take a resting value first. Do each activity for the same set time, and rest between activities until the pulse is back to normal. Keep the same person, the same type and intensity of exercise, and the same conditions.
  • Reaction time: hold a ruler at zero between the person's thumb and finger, always with the same gap. Drop it without warning and record the distance it falls before it is caught. Repeat, find the mean, then use the formula to get the reaction time. Repeat for each student.
  • Pupil size: start in a dark room, then shine a light or use lamps of different brightness. Wait for the pupil to adjust, photograph the eye, and measure the pupil diameter with a ruler.
  • Hearing range: play sounds from a signal generator and speaker, raising the frequency until the person can no longer hear it, and record that maximum. Test people of a range of ages, at the same distance from the speaker, with the same sex and no ear problems.
  • Stereoscopic vision: throw a ball from a fixed distance many times (for example 10) and count the catches with both eyes open, then with one eye covered by a patch. Use the same ball, thrower and lighting.
  • Sweating rate: press absorbent paper or cotton wool on a fixed area of skin, weigh it before and after, or time how long cobalt chloride paper takes to change colour.
  • Urine volume: collect all the urine for a set time on a cold day and on a hot day, and measure the volume with a measuring cylinder. Keep the person, the volume drunk, the food and the activity the same, then compare.
  • Drug or diet trials: use two groups matched for age, sex and health. Give the treatment (statins, or vitamin B in the diet) to one group only; the other group gets none or a placebo. After a set time, compare a measurement such as blood cholesterol or health.
  • Survey data over years: choose a sample of people, record who takes statins, take blood samples to measure cholesterol, and repeat each year.

Before you begin

  • Effects of exercise on heart rate.
  • The iris reflex and hearing range.
  • Control variables and reliability.
1

The method

  1. Say what you change (activity, light intensity, age, treatment) and give its values.

    Why: Changing the factor is a marking point.

  2. Say exactly how you measure the response: where, with what, and when.

    Why: Schemes credit the measuring method separately from the measurement.

  3. Take a baseline or use a control group.

    Why: Resting values and untreated groups give a comparison and earn marks.

  4. Name at least two human control variables.

    Why: Same person, same exercise, same food are often credited.

  5. Repeat (or test many people), calculate a mean, and compare the results.

    Why: Repeats and comparison are usually the last marks.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Not taking a resting pulse before exercise.
  • ✗
    Saying 'measure the pulse' without saying how (fingers on wrist or neck, monitor).
  • ✗
    Doing the next activity straight away without letting the pulse return to resting.
  • ✗
    In a drug trial, giving everyone the drug, so there is no comparison group.
  • ✗
    Measuring 'the amount of sweat' with no method for measuring it.
  • ✗
    Testing catching with both eyes only, so there is nothing to compare with one eye.
  • ✗
    Testing one person of each age for hearing, so one unusual person changes the result.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. The graph shows pulse rate against time spent exercising, from 0 to 100 seconds.
  2. So the independent variable is time exercising and the dependent variable is pulse rate.
  3. Start with a resting pulse, fingers on the wrist.
  4. Exercise for a set time, then count the pulse straight away.
  5. Rest until the pulse is back to resting, then exercise for a longer time.
  6. Add controls (same person, same exercise) and repeats with a mean.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

The student sits still for 5 minutes and then measures the resting pulse by placing two fingers on the wrist and counting the beats for 15 seconds, then multiplying by 4. The student then does step-ups for 20 seconds and measures the pulse again immediately after stopping. The student rests until the pulse returns to the resting rate. This is repeated with exercise times of 40, 60, 80 and 100 seconds. The same person does the same exercise at the same speed each time. Each exercise time is repeated three times and a mean pulse rate is calculated.

How the marks are earned

Marks for: a resting pulse before exercise; fingers on the wrist or neck (or a monitor); doing the exercise; recording pulse afterwards; waiting until pulse is back to normal; repeating; naming a control variable such as same person or same exercise intensity.
