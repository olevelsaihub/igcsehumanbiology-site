Knowledge card

Explain antigen-antibody specificity and how monoclonal antibodies are made

Written
  • An antigen is a chemical, usually a protein, on the surface of a cell or pathogen. The immune system uses antigens to recognise foreign material.
  • An antibody is a protein made by lymphocytes (a type of white blood cell). It is not a cell.
  • Each antibody has a binding site (variable region) with one particular shape. Different antibodies have different shapes.
  • The binding site is complementary to one antigen only, so each antibody binds to just one type of antigen. This is why the reaction is specific.
  • If the body treats its own antigens as foreign (autoimmune disease), antibodies attach to its own cells and damage them, as in coeliac disease.
  • Proteins from another species, such as insulin taken from animals, can be recognised as foreign antigens and cause an immune response or allergy.
  • Monoclonal antibodies: inject a mouse with the antigen; take lymphocytes from its spleen; fuse them with tumour (myeloma) cells to make hybridoma cells; grow (clone) the hybridomas, which make large amounts of one antibody.
  • After a phagocyte digests a pathogen, it can show the pathogen's antigens on its own surface. This helps lymphocytes recognise the antigen, so the immune response is faster and stronger.

Before you begin

  • Proteins have specific 3D shapes, like enzyme active sites.
  • White blood cells include phagocytes and lymphocytes with different jobs.
  • Cells have proteins on their cell membrane.
1

The method

  1. Identify the antigen (the target) and the antibody (the protein made in response).

    Why: Swapping the two terms loses marks in every member of this type.

  2. Say who makes the antibody: lymphocytes.

    Why: This is often a separate mark and shows the correct cell.

  3. Describe the shape: each antibody has its own binding-site shape that is complementary to one antigen.

    Why: The complementary-shape idea is the key mark for specificity.

  4. Finish with the action: the antibody binds to that antigen only, which leads to the target being destroyed or damaged.

    Why: Binding is a separate creditworthy step.

  5. For monoclonal antibodies, write the steps in order: inject antigen, take spleen lymphocytes, fuse with tumour cells, hybridoma, clone, collect antibody.

    Why: Each step in sequence earns one mark.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Saying antibodies are cells that engulf bacteria. Phagocytes engulf; antibodies are proteins.
  • ✗
    Saying an antibody has 'the same shape' as the antigen instead of a complementary shape.
  • ✗
    Mixing up antigen and antibody, or antibody and antibiotic.
  • ✗
    Writing only 'the antibody is specific' without explaining the shape of the binding site.
  • ✗
    Leaving out the fusion with tumour cells, or the hybridoma, when describing monoclonal antibody production.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Command word 'Explain', 3 marks: I need three linked ideas, not just the word 'specific'.
  2. Start with the structure: each antibody has a binding site, called the variable region.
  3. Next: this variable region has a particular shape, and it differs from one type of antibody to another.
  4. Then the key link: the shape is complementary to one antigen.
  5. Finish with the result: so the antibody can attach only to that antigen.
  6. Check I have not called the antibody a cell, and that I said 'complementary', not 'the same'.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Each antibody has a binding site at the end of its arms, called the variable region. The variable region of each type of antibody has its own unique shape, so antibodies differ from one another. This shape fits (is complementary to) the shape of one antigen only. So an antibody can attach only to the matching antigen and to no other antigen.

How the marks are earned

Marks go to: the antibody having a variable region or receptor; that region having a particular shape that varies between antibodies; the shape being complementary to the antigen; and the antibody binding to the antigen. Any three earn full marks. Saying the shapes are 'the same' is not credited.
