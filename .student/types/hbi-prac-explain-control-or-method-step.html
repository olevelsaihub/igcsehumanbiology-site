Describe a control or explain why a step is in a method

Control tubes, discs or beakers without the active ingredient, and justified processing steps such as blotting, rinsing or using percentage change.

Knowledge card

Describe a control or explain why a step is in a method

Written
  • A control experiment is set up the same way but without the factor being tested (no antibiotic, no enzyme, boiled enzyme, or water instead).
  • A control shows that any change in the test is caused by the tested factor, and not by something else in the method.
  • A boiled enzyme is denatured, so it shows what happens with no active enzyme present.
  • Blotting potato cylinders dry before weighing removes surface liquid, which would add extra mass and give a wrong reading.
  • Percentage change in mass allows fair comparison when the pieces start with different masses.
  • Rinsing the outside of Visking tubing removes any solution spilt on it, so it cannot contaminate the water outside.
  • A tube with everything except the enzyme (for example fat and bile salts only) shows that the other substance on its own does not cause the change.
  • In the control, replace the enzyme with the same volume of water or boiled enzyme, so the total volume in each tube stays the same.

Before you begin

  • Enzymes are denatured by high temperature.
  • Antibiotics and antibacterial agents stop bacterial growth.
  • Osmosis changes the mass of plant tissue.
1

The method

  1. For a control, copy the test set-up and remove only the tested factor (replace it with water or boiled enzyme).

    Why: This is the description mark, and naming what replaces it is often credited.

  2. Say that the other conditions and volumes stay the same as the test.

    Why: Some schemes give a mark for the same volume or same overall volume.

  3. Say the expected control result, e.g. bacteria grow right up to the disc.

    Why: Three-mark Explain questions credit the expected result.

  4. Say what the control proves: the tested factor caused the change.

    Why: This is the purpose mark.

  5. For a method step, name the error it prevents and how that error would change the reading.

    Why: Two-mark questions pair the action with its effect on the result.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Describing a control as 'keep everything the same' without saying what is left out.
  • ✗
    Mixing up a control experiment with a control variable.
  • ✗
    Leaving out the bacteria from the control dish, so it does not show what happens without antibiotic.
  • ✗
    Saying blotting is 'to make it fair' without saying that surface water adds to the mass.
  • ✗
    Saying percentage change is 'more accurate' rather than allowing comparison between pieces of different starting mass.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. The test discs contain antibiotic X or Y. The factor being tested is the antibiotic.
  2. So the control is the same disc of filter paper with no antibiotic, soaked in water instead.
  3. Expected result: bacteria grow up to the disc, with no clear zone.
  4. Purpose: if only X and Y give clear zones, the antibiotics caused them.
  5. Three marks, three ideas: set-up, result, conclusion.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Place a filter paper disc soaked in sterile water, with no antibiotic, on the same agar plate. Bacteria should grow right up to this disc, so there is no clear zone around it. This shows that the clear zones around discs X and Y are caused by the antibiotics and not by the filter paper.

How the marks are earned

One mark for a disc of filter paper without antibiotic; one for stating that bacteria grow normally with no clear zone; one for saying this shows whether the antibiotic is responsible for the effect.
