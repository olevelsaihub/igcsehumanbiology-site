Knowledge card

Use genetic terms: alleles, genotypes, carriers and blood-group genotypes

Written
  • A gene is a section of DNA that controls a characteristic. An allele is a different (alternative) version of the same gene.
  • Dominant: an allele that shows its effect even when only one copy is present.
  • Recessive: an allele that only shows its effect when two copies are present (homozygous); in a heterozygote it is hidden by the dominant allele.
  • Homozygous means two identical alleles (AA or aa); heterozygous means two different alleles (Aa).
  • Genotype is the alleles a person has (such as Pp); phenotype is the characteristic you can see or measure (such as extra fingers or blood group B).
  • Co-dominant: when two different alleles are both present, both are fully expressed in the phenotype, as in blood group AB (IA IB).
  • ABO blood groups have three alleles: IA and IB are co-dominant, and IO is recessive to both. Group A = IA IA or IA IO; group B = IB IB or IB IO; group AB = IA IB only; group O = IO IO only.
  • A carrier is heterozygous: one normal dominant allele and one faulty recessive allele. The dominant allele masks the faulty one, so a carrier has no symptoms but can pass the faulty allele to children. In an X-linked condition such as DMD, a carrier is a female with the faulty allele on only one X chromosome.
  • Cystic fibrosis and sickle cell anaemia are caused by a mutation that makes a recessive allele. A person has the condition only if they inherit two copies (homozygous recessive).
  • Two parents without the condition can have an affected child if both are carriers: each parent passes one recessive allele, so the child gets one from each parent and is homozygous recessive.

Before you begin

  • Genes are sections of DNA on chromosomes that code for a characteristic.
  • Diploid cells have two copies of each gene, one from each parent; gametes carry one copy.
  • A mutation is a change in DNA that can make a new allele.
  • ABO blood groups A, B, AB and O.
1

The method

  1. Identify the exact term or idea being tested.

    Why: Similar terms (gene and allele, genotype and phenotype, carrier and affected) are easy to swap.

  2. Give the definition with its key qualifier, such as 'only when two copies are present' or 'both present and both expressed'.

    Why: The qualifier is what earns the mark; a vague definition does not.

  3. For a two-mark definition, give the condition and the result as two ideas.

    Why: Each idea is credited separately.

  4. For blood-group genotypes, list every pair of alleles that gives that group, using the I symbols. Remember IO is hidden by IA or IB.

    Why: Group A and group B each have two genotypes and each one can be a separate mark.

  5. For carriers, say they are heterozygous, that the dominant allele masks the recessive one, and that two recessive alleles are needed for the condition.

    Why: Masking and the need for two copies are the two credited ideas.

  6. For how a recessive condition is inherited, say the parents are carriers (or affected) and that the child receives one recessive allele from each parent.

    Why: Both the parents' genotypes and 'one allele from each parent' earn marks.

  7. Add a short genotype example if it helps (IA IB gives group AB; Ff is a carrier).

    Why: An example confirms the meaning and is often allowed.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Defining an allele as 'a chromosome' or 'a pair of chromosomes'.
  • ✗
    Calling a recessive allele 'weaker' or saying the dominant allele 'overpowers' it, instead of saying it is masked and only shows when two copies are present.
  • ✗
    Defining co-dominant as 'both alleles are dominant' without saying both are expressed together.
  • ✗
    Giving only IA IA for group A (or only IB IB for group B) and missing the heterozygous genotype with IO.
  • ✗
    Mixing up genotype and phenotype, or calling Pp homozygous, or saying a carrier has a mild form of the condition.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. The term is co-dominant and the context is ABO blood groups: IA and IB are co-dominant.
  2. Two marks, so I need two ideas.
  3. Idea 1, the condition: both different alleles are present in the genotype (IA IB).
  4. Idea 2, the result: both alleles show their effect equally, so the person is group AB.
  5. I must not say one allele hides the other.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

When a person has two different alleles, both of them are present in the genotype (for example IA and IB). Both alleles are expressed equally in the phenotype, so neither hides the other; IA IB gives blood group AB.

How the marks are earned

One mark for the idea that both different alleles are present together; one mark for both being expressed (equally). Saying only 'both are dominant' would not earn the expression mark.
