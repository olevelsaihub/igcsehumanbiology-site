Knowledge card

Define specialised cells, tissues and organs and identify human tissues

Written
  • A specialised cell has features that make it suited to one particular job, for example a neurone or a sperm.
  • A tissue is a group of similar cells that work together to do one job.
  • An organ is made of several different tissues working together to do a job, for example the heart.
  • Squamous epithelium is made of flat, irregular cells such as cheek cells; ciliated epithelium has cilia and lines the trachea.
  • Cardiac muscle (in the heart wall) is striped and branched, with one central nucleus in each cell.
  • Voluntary (skeletal) muscle is striped and unbranched with many nuclei; involuntary (smooth) muscle is not striped, has tapered cells with one nucleus, and is found in the gut wall, blood vessels, uterus and iris.
  • Other human tissues include blood, bone, nervous tissue and connective tissue.
  • Cheek cell compared with trachea cell: a cheek cell is flat and irregular in shape, has no cilia and has its nucleus in the centre; a trachea cell is tall and oblong (column-shaped), has cilia on its free surface, and its nucleus sits near the base.
  • The heart wall is made mainly of cardiac muscle tissue. Involuntary (smooth) muscle is found in the gut wall (oesophagus, small intestine), arteries and veins, the uterus, the iris and sphincters.

  • The appearance of cheek cells and ciliated cells under the microscope.

The method

  1. For a definition, write two linked parts: what it is made of, and that it does a particular job.

    Why: Schemes give one mark for the make-up and one for the shared function.

  2. For 'how are cells organised into organs', write the two steps in order: cells form tissues, tissues form organs.

    Why: Each step is a separate mark.

  3. For tissue names, give the full name with the type word: squamous epithelium, ciliated epithelium, cardiac muscle.

    Why: A vague answer like 'a muscle' or 'a bone' is rejected.

  4. For muscle structure, describe three features for each type: striped or not, branched or not, number of nuclei.

    Why: Each type has its own marks, so you must describe all three types.

  5. For a cheek-trachea comparison, write each difference as a pair about one feature: shape (irregular or oblong), cilia (absent or present), position of nucleus (central or near the base).

    Why: A paired contrast shows the examiner both sides and earns the mark.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Defining a tissue as 'a group of cells' and leaving out that they are similar and share a job.
  • ✗
    Writing 'a muscle' or 'a bone' as a type of tissue; these are organs.
  • ✗
    Writing 'cells form organs' and missing the tissue level in between.
  • ✗
    Swapping the features of voluntary and involuntary muscle, for example giving smooth muscle stripes.
  • ✗
    Calling the cheek lining 'ciliated epithelium'.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. The question asks how body cells are organised to form organs. There are 2 marks, so I need two steps.
  2. Step 1: cells of the same kind group together and work together. This group is a tissue.
  3. Step 2: several different tissues work together. This makes an organ.
  4. I can add an example, like the heart, but the two steps are what score.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Cells of the same type group together and cooperate to form a tissue. Several different tissues then join and cooperate to make an organ, such as the heart.

How the marks are earned

One mark for the idea that similar cells group and cooperate as a tissue. One mark for the idea that different tissues cooperate to form an organ. Missing the tissue step loses a mark.
