Knowledge card

Explain how the placenta, cord and hormones support a fetus

Written
  • The placenta is where the mother's blood and the fetus's blood come very close but do not mix. Substances diffuse across it.
  • Glucose, amino acids, other nutrients and oxygen pass from mother to fetus; carbon dioxide and urea pass from fetus to mother.
  • The umbilical cord joins the fetus to the placenta. Its blood vessels carry nutrients and oxygen to the fetus and carry waste back.
  • Oxygen and glucose let the fetus respire aerobically, releasing energy for growth. Less supply means less respiration and less growth, so a lower birth mass.
  • Harmful substances (some drugs, alcohol, nicotine, some medicines) can also cross the placenta and damage the growing fetus; some also pass into breast milk.
  • Progesterone keeps the uterus lining thick during pregnancy. Early on it comes from the corpus luteum (kept alive by hCG from the embryo); later the placenta makes it.
  • Amniotic fluid surrounds the fetus and protects it from bumps.

Before you begin

  • Aerobic respiration uses glucose and oxygen to release energy.
  • Diffusion moves substances from high to low concentration.
  • The role of progesterone in the menstrual cycle.
1

The method

  1. Name the substance and its direction: from the mother's blood, across the placenta, along the cord to the fetus (or the reverse for waste).

    Why: The route and named substances are separate credit points.

  2. Say what the substance is used for: oxygen and glucose for respiration, amino acids for growth.

    Why: Linking supply to fetal respiration or growth earns the consequence mark.

  3. Finish with the effect on the fetus: more or less growth, lower birth mass, or damage to development.

    Why: Questions about birth mass or harm always credit the final outcome.

  4. For hormone parts, follow the chain: hCG keeps the corpus luteum, which makes progesterone, which keeps the lining.

    Why: Each link in the hormone chain is credited.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Saying the mother's blood mixes with the baby's blood in the placenta.
  • ✗
    Writing 'the twins get less food' without naming glucose or oxygen and linking to respiration and energy.
  • ✗
    Saying the placenta makes energy for the fetus.
  • ✗
    For drugs, writing 'it affects the baby' without saying it crosses the placenta and harms growth or development.
  • ✗
    Saying the corpus luteum keeps the lining itself, instead of saying it makes progesterone that keeps the lining.

Worked explanation

  1. The twins share one placenta, so the important idea is sharing a supply.
  2. First say what the placenta normally does: it brings glucose and oxygen from the mother's blood.
  3. Two fetuses share the same supply, so each gets less glucose and oxygen.
  4. Less glucose and oxygen means less aerobic respiration in the fetal cells.
  5. Less respiration releases less energy, so there is less growth and a lower mass at birth.
  6. Three marks, so I make sure at least three of these links appear clearly.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

The placenta passes glucose and oxygen from the mother's blood to the fetus. When two twins share one placenta, each twin receives less glucose and less oxygen. So each fetus carries out less aerobic respiration, and less energy is released in its cells. With less energy available, less growth happens, so the twins are born with a lower mass.

How the marks are earned

Any three of: the placenta supplying glucose or oxygen from the mother; the twins sharing this so each gets less; less aerobic respiration; less energy released so less growth. 'Nutrients' is allowed in place of glucose for the first two ideas.
