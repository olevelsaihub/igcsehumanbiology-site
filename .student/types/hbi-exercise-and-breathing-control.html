Knowledge card

Explain heart and breathing changes in exercise and how carbon dioxide controls breathing

Written
  • During exercise muscles contract more, so they need more energy. They respire faster and need more oxygen and glucose.
  • Heart rate rises so more blood flows to the muscles, bringing oxygen and glucose and removing carbon dioxide faster.
  • Blood is moved away from the gut, liver and kidneys and towards the skeletal muscles during exercise.
  • Faster respiration makes more carbon dioxide. Chemoreceptors in the aorta and carotid arteries detect the rise (and the fall in blood pH).
  • The medulla oblongata in the brain then sends impulses to raise breathing rate and depth, and heart rate, to remove the carbon dioxide.
  • If oxygen supply is too low, muscles respire anaerobically and make lactic acid. After exercise, extra oxygen (the oxygen debt) is needed to break down the lactic acid, so breathing and heart rate stay high for a while.
  • Adrenaline raises heart rate and blood pressure, widens the air passages so breathing is faster and deeper, sends more blood to the muscles, brain, heart and lungs, and makes the liver and muscles convert glycogen to glucose for more aerobic respiration.
  • The vagus nerve carries impulses from the brain to the heart that slow the heart rate. If a drug such as atropine stops the vagus nerve working, the heart rate rises.
  • Breathing rate is not the only process that rises in exercise: respiration (metabolism), blood pressure and sweating also increase.
  • Breathing air that contains more carbon dioxide raises the carbon dioxide in the blood. Chemoreceptors detect this, so both breathing rate and tidal volume increase to remove the extra carbon dioxide.

The method

  1. Describe the change first, with a figure from the graph or table if given.

    Why: Several schemes give a mark for stating the trend, for example pulse rises as exercise time rises.

  2. Link it to muscle needs: more contraction, more energy, faster aerobic respiration.

    Why: The respiration mark is in almost every scheme for this type.

  3. Say what the extra blood flow or breathing supplies: more oxygen and glucose, more carbon dioxide removed.

    Why: The scheme wants the purpose of the change.

  4. For control questions, give detector then effector: chemoreceptors detect carbon dioxide, the medulla raises breathing rate.

    Why: Detection and response are separate marks.

  5. For recovery, mention lactic acid and oxygen debt.

    Why: Recovery marks need anaerobic respiration and its repayment.

  6. For 'how does faster breathing help the muscles', write about oxygen: more oxygen taken in, more aerobic respiration, more energy, and oxygen to break down lactic acid.

    Why: In this kind of question the scheme ignores carbon dioxide points, so oxygen and lactic acid ideas earn the marks.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Saying muscles 'make energy' instead of releasing energy by respiration.
  • ✗
    Saying oxygen level is detected to control breathing; at this level teach carbon dioxide detection.
  • ✗
    Not naming where the chemoreceptors are: the specification names the aorta and the carotid arteries, so give these.
  • ✗
    Writing 'heart beats faster' with no reason about oxygen, glucose or carbon dioxide.
  • ✗
    Ignoring lactic acid when explaining why rates stay high after exercise.
  • ✗
    When asked how a faster breathing rate helps working muscles, writing only about removing carbon dioxide.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. The graph: pulse rises from about 70 to about 140 beats per minute as exercise time rises from 0 to 100 seconds.
  2. Why? Muscles contract more, so they need more energy.
  3. The heart beats faster, so more blood flows to the muscles each minute.
  4. This blood brings more oxygen and glucose for aerobic respiration.
  5. It also carries away the extra carbon dioxide, and less lactic acid builds up.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

As the time spent exercising increases, the pulse rate increases, from about 70 to about 140 beats per minute. The heart pumps faster so more blood flows to the muscles. This delivers more oxygen and glucose to the muscle cells, so they can carry out more aerobic respiration to release the energy needed for contraction. The faster blood flow also removes more carbon dioxide, and less lactic acid builds up because less anaerobic respiration is needed.

How the marks are earned

Marks for: pulse rises as exercise time rises; more blood flow to muscles; more oxygen or glucose delivered; more aerobic respiration; more carbon dioxide removed or less lactic acid. Any four give full marks.
