Knowledge card

Explain water movement by osmosis in body contexts

Written
  • Water moves by osmosis from a higher water potential (dilute) to a lower water potential (concentrated) across a partially permeable membrane.
  • Adding solute, such as glucose or other dissolved products, lowers the water potential of that side.
  • If the cell has a lower water potential than the fluid around it, water enters the cell and it swells; animal cells may burst.
  • If the blood or gut contents have a lower water potential than the cells, water leaves the cells; the cells shrink.
  • In diabetes, high blood glucose draws water out of cells into the blood, and the extra water leaves as urine.
  • To find the concentration of cell contents, plot percentage change in mass against solution concentration and read where the change is zero.
  • After dehydration (for example heavy urination after alcohol), the plasma has a lower water potential. Red blood cells lose water by osmosis and become distorted, so they carry less oxygen; plasma volume falls and the blood becomes thicker and harder to pump.
  • In lactose intolerance, undigested lactose stays in the gut and lowers the water potential there. Water moves out of the body cells into the gut by osmosis, causing diarrhoea, so food passes quickly and less calcium is absorbed.
  • In cellular oedema, too much water enters the cells, so they swell and may burst.

Before you begin

  • Definition of osmosis and of water potential.
  • The cell membrane is partially permeable.
  • Structure of blood and tissue fluid; kidneys remove excess water as urine.
1

The method

  1. Name the process: osmosis.

    Why: Many schemes give one mark just for naming osmosis.

  2. Compare the water potential on each side of the membrane: which side has more solute, so a lower water potential?

    Why: The scheme credits the correct comparison of water potential.

  3. State the direction: water moves from higher to lower water potential, across the partially permeable membrane.

    Why: The direction mark needs both the gradient and the membrane idea.

  4. Give the consequence asked for: cells swell or burst, cells shrink, more urine, diarrhoea, thicker blood.

    Why: Suggest and explain questions save their last marks for the effect on the body.

  5. For data questions, use percentage change in mass and find the zero-change concentration on a graph.

    Why: The point of no net water movement is where both water potentials are equal.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Saying water moves from a low to a high water potential.
  • ✗
    Saying 'glucose moves out of the cell' when the question is about water.
  • ✗
    Writing that red blood cells 'burst' when they are in a concentrated solution (they shrink).
  • ✗
    Using 'water concentration' loosely instead of water potential.
  • ✗
    In potato data, reading the concentration with the biggest mass change instead of zero change.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Too much water enters body cells. Water moves into cells by osmosis, so I name osmosis first.
  2. For water to go in, the inside of the cell must have the lower water potential. So the tissue fluid has the higher water potential.
  3. Water moves from the higher water potential to the lower water potential, down the water potential gradient.
  4. It crosses the cell membrane, which is partially permeable. That completes the chain for 3 marks.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Water enters the body cells by osmosis. The cytoplasm of the cells has a lower water potential than the tissue fluid around them, so the tissue fluid has the higher water potential. Water therefore moves across the partially permeable cell membrane from the higher water potential in the tissue fluid to the lower water potential inside the cells, down a water potential gradient.

How the marks are earned

One mark for naming osmosis. One mark for comparing water potentials correctly (lower inside the cells, higher in the tissue fluid). One mark for the direction: water goes from high to low water potential. The reverse direction (low to high) earns nothing.
