Use aseptic technique to test antibacterial agents on agar

Petri-dish investigations of antibiotics, cleaners or disinfectants: aseptic transfer, contamination control, culture safety and judging effectiveness by clear zones or colonies.

Type 12 Investigations HBI-PRAC-ASEPTIC-AGAR-PLATE-TEST

★★★☆☆ · 5 of 36 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Difficult.

Students must plan a sequence and give a reason for each sterile step. Needs understanding of contamination, pathogens and how agents affect growth. No calculation. Contexts (pit latrines, MRSA, garlic) are unusual, but the technique is standard. Combines aseptic technique, safety, control variables and measurement.

Knowledge card

Use aseptic technique to test antibacterial agents on agar

Written
  • Aseptic technique stops unwanted microorganisms getting into the culture and stops the culture escaping.
  • Sterilise the inoculating loop by heating it in a Bunsen flame until red hot, then let it cool; use sterile agar and sterile Petri dishes.
  • Open the lid only a little and for a short time, work near a Bunsen flame, and flame the neck of the culture bottle.
  • Tape the lid on, and do not open the dish after incubation, so bacteria cannot reach people.
  • Incubate at 25 °C or below, so harmful bacteria that grow at body temperature are less likely to grow.
  • An antibacterial agent kills or stops bacteria around it, leaving a clear zone. A larger clear zone (or fewer colonies) means a more effective agent.
  • To test a cleaner on a surface: wash the surface with one solution, wait a set time, wipe it with a sterile swab, spread the swab on agar, incubate and count the colonies. Repeat with the second solution and compare.
  • To tell MRSA from ordinary S. aureus, grow the sample on agar with antibiotic discs. MRSA still grows next to methicillin, so there is little or no clear zone.

Before you begin

  • Bacteria reproduce quickly in warm conditions with nutrients.
  • Antibiotics and antiseptics kill or stop bacteria.
  • Some bacteria are pathogens.
1

The method

  1. Prepare: sterile agar plates, loop sterilised in a flame, same volume and concentration of each agent.

    Why: Each sterile step and each control earns marks.

  2. Transfer bacteria quickly with the lid lifted a little; add discs or the sample.

    Why: Schemes credit fast transfer and minimal opening of the lid.

  3. Tape the lid, incubate at 25 °C for a set time.

    Why: Sealing and the temperature limit are separate marks, each with a safety reason.

  4. Measure the clear zone diameter or count colonies; repeat and compare.

    Why: Planning questions give marks for the measurement and comparison.

  5. In Explain questions, give the reason after every step.

    Why: Some schemes limit marks if no reasons are given.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Writing 'keep everything clean' instead of a named step such as flaming the loop.
  • ✗
    Incubating at 37 °C, which encourages human pathogens.
  • ✗
    Opening the lid after incubation to look closely at the colonies, which lets bacteria escape.
  • ✗
    Judging effectiveness by 'how much bacteria' without saying measure the clear zone or count colonies.
  • ✗
    In a design answer, forgetting to keep the volume and concentration of each solution the same.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Design, 7 marks, full sentences. I need at least seven separate details.
  2. The factor changed is the antibacterial solution (two types). Control: same volume and same concentration.
  3. How to test: wash the slab with one solution, wait a set time, then sample the slab.
  4. Sampling must be aseptic: sterile swab or loop, onto sterile nutrient agar.
  5. Incubate for a stated time. Then count colonies: fewer colonies means a better solution.
  6. Repeat with the second solution and compare. I end with repeats for reliability.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

The scientist uses the same volume (50 cm³) of each antibacterial solution, at the same concentration. One area of the concrete slab is washed with solution A and is left for 30 minutes. A sterile swab, or an inoculating loop heated in a Bunsen flame and cooled, is rubbed over a fixed area of the washed slab. The sample is spread onto sterile nutrient agar in a Petri dish, and the lid is taped on. The dish is incubated at 25 °C for 3 days. The same steps are repeated with solution B on a similar area of the slab. The number of bacterial colonies on each dish is counted, and the solution whose dish has fewer colonies is the more effective one. The whole investigation is repeated and the mean numbers of colonies are compared.

How the marks are earned

Marks come from: equal volumes; equal concentrations; washing the slab with one solution; a stated waiting time; a sterile loop or swab; moving the sample onto agar; incubating for a stated time; counting colonies or measuring clear zones; doing the same with the second solution; comparing the results. Any seven score full marks.
