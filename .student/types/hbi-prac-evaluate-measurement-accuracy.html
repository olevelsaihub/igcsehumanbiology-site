Identify a measurement weakness and improve accuracy

Methods with subjective scoring, uneven bubble counts, too few or widely spaced intervals, or a contaminated reading.

★★★☆☆ · 5 of 36 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Medium.

Students must judge what is weak in the data and design a better measurement. Needs the meaning of accuracy and some enzyme knowledge. No calculation. Methods are unusual (bubble scores, cheese making) but the flaws are common. Combines accuracy, reliability and enzyme optimum ideas.

Knowledge card

Identify a measurement weakness and improve accuracy

Written
  • Accurate results are close to the true value.
  • Results based on judgement, such as a 0 to 5 score for bubbles or a colour by eye, are subjective and not accurate.
  • Counting bubbles is inaccurate because bubbles are different sizes; measure the volume of gas with a gas syringe or measuring cylinder instead.
  • To find an optimum, use more values of the independent variable with smaller gaps, especially near the peak.
  • An unexpected result, such as distilled water testing positive for protein, can come from dirty or contaminated equipment.

Before you begin

  • Enzymes have an optimum temperature and pH.
  • Tests for protein and other food tests.
  • The difference between qualitative and quantitative data.
1

The method

  1. Find the weak measurement: a score, a bubble count, a colour judged by eye, or temperatures far apart.

    Why: Naming the specific flaw earns the first mark.

  2. Replace it with a number from an instrument: volume of gas in a gas syringe, time with a stopwatch.

    Why: Schemes credit both the quantity measured and the apparatus.

  3. For an optimum, add more values with smaller intervals between the two highest results.

    Why: The true peak may lie between the values tested.

  4. If the question also says reliable, add repeats and a mean.

    Why: Some schemes give a separate mark for repeats.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Saying 'use better equipment' without naming the equipment or the quantity.
  • ✗
    Giving only 'repeat it' when the question asks about accuracy.
  • ✗
    Suggesting temperatures outside the useful range instead of between the tested values.
  • ✗
    Saying the student 'used the wrong reagent' when the other results show the reagent worked.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. The student scores bubbles from 0 to 5 by eye. This is a judgement, so it is not accurate.
  2. Better: measure the volume of oxygen produced.
  3. Apparatus: a gas syringe connected to a sealed flask.
  4. The test was done only once, so for reliability I add repeats and a mean.
  5. Three marks: volume of oxygen, gas syringe, repeats.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Measure the volume of oxygen given off in a fixed time, for example 2 minutes, instead of scoring the bubbles by eye. Collect the oxygen in a gas syringe connected to a sealed flask. Repeat each condition three times and calculate a mean.

How the marks are earned

One mark for measuring the volume of oxygen, one for naming suitable apparatus such as a gas syringe, and one for doing repeats.
