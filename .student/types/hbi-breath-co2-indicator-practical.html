Pearson Edexcel · International GCSE Human Biology · 4HB1

Set up and explain the indicator practical comparing carbon dioxide in breath

Two-tube limewater or indicator apparatus with a mouthpiece: drawing and using it, naming the indicator, and explaining results for inspired, expired, rest or exercise air.

Type 3 Respiration HBI-BREATH-CO2-INDICATOR-PRACTICAL

★★★☆☆ · 5 of 36 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Medium.

Simple cause and effect between CO2 level and indicator change. Basic gas composition facts and respiration. No calculation. Apparatus differs (test tube, conical flasks with T piece, boiling tubes), so the layout must be worked out. Combines practical drawing, method writing and explanation.

Knowledge card

Set up and explain the indicator practical comparing carbon dioxide in breath

Written

Expired air has about 4% carbon dioxide, inspired air about 0.04%. Limewater goes cloudy faster with expired air because cells make carbon dioxide in respiration.

Exam tipOnly one tube per flask dips in.

Ends above the liquid

Write it as
The short tube ends above the limewater.

Expired air bubbles through

Write it as
Breathe out through the mouthpiece into the tube whose long tube dips in.

Room air bubbles through

Write it as
Breathing in pulls room air through the other tube's long tube.

4

The method

  1. Draw

    Mouthpiece to both tubes; one long, one short in each flask.

  2. Breathe

    Breathe out through one tube, in through the other.

  3. Fair test

    Time it or use the same number of breaths; fresh limewater.

  4. Explain

    More CO2 in expired air, from respiration.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Both tubes dipping

    Liquid could be sucked into the mouth.

  • ✗
    No CO2 in inspired air

    It has about 0.04%.

  • ✗
    Lungs make CO2

    Body cells make it in respiration.

  • ✗
    No timing

    Add a time or a number of breaths.

Worked explanation

  1. Explain the difference between the results: inhaled air versus exhaled air in limewater.
  2. What will be seen? Limewater with exhaled air goes cloudy faster.
  3. Why? Exhaled air contains more carbon dioxide.
  4. Where does that extra carbon dioxide come from? Respiration in body cells.
  5. Three marks: result, which air has more CO2, and the reason.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Exhaled air contains more carbon dioxide than inhaled air (about 4% compared with 0.04%). So the limewater with exhaled air turns cloudy more quickly, in less time. The extra carbon dioxide is made by respiration in body cells.

How the marks are earned

One mark for exhaled air having more carbon dioxide, one for the limewater taking less time to go cloudy with exhaled air, and one for respiration as the source of the carbon dioxide.
