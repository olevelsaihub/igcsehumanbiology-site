Knowledge card

State the variables to control in an investigation

Written
  • A control variable is a factor kept the same in every test, so that only the independent variable can cause the change in results.
  • Never give the factor being changed (independent) or the thing being measured (dependent) as a control variable.
  • Name the factor exactly: 'volume of enzyme', 'temperature', 'mass of food'. The word 'amount' is often refused.
  • Enzyme work: temperature, pH, volume and concentration of enzyme, volume and concentration of substrate.
  • Food energy: mass of food, volume of water, distance of the food from the tube, type of tube.
  • Investigations on people: same person (or same age and sex), same type, intensity and length of exercise, same food and drink, same clothing and room temperature.
  • Tissue and cube work: same size or surface area of each piece (potato cylinder, agar cube, strip of film), same type of potato or food, same volume and concentration of each solution, same volume of indicator.
  • Microbe and yoghurt work: same incubation temperature, same type and volume of milk, same type and volume of starter culture, same type of agar.
  • Measuring set-ups: same time between readings, same filter in a colorimeter, same size of clot or width of straw in a flow model.

Before you begin

  • What independent and dependent variables are.
  • Factors that affect enzyme activity: temperature, pH, concentration.
  • How a fair test works.
1

The method

  1. Find the independent variable (what is changed) and the dependent variable (what is measured).

    Why: These two cannot be control variables, so you avoid giving an answer worth zero.

  2. Read the method again and list every other quantity in it: volumes, concentrations, masses, sizes, times, temperature, pH.

    Why: The scheme usually credits quantities that already appear in the method.

  3. Add factors the method forgets, such as temperature or pH, if they would change the result.

    Why: Temperature is credited in almost every bench investigation.

  4. Write each answer as a precise measurable factor, one per line, e.g. 'volume of milk'.

    Why: Vague answers like 'same conditions' or 'amount' score nothing.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Writing 'amount of food' or 'amount of enzyme' instead of 'mass' or 'volume'.
  • ✗
    Giving the independent variable, for example 'temperature' when the investigation is testing temperature.
  • ✗
    Writing 'same conditions', 'same equipment' or 'fair test' without naming a factor.
  • ✗
    Giving two answers that are the same idea, such as 'volume of water' and 'mass of water', and expecting two marks.
  • ✗
    Listing the time already fixed by the question when it says 'other than' that factor.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. The independent variable is the concentration of phenolphthalein phosphate, because six different concentrations are made.
  2. The dependent variable is the colorimeter reading of pink colour.
  3. The volumes (5.0 cm³ and 1.0 cm³) are already fixed, so I look for other factors that change enzyme activity.
  4. Enzymes are affected by temperature and pH, and by how much enzyme is present.
  5. So I write three precise factors: temperature, pH, concentration of phosphatase.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

1 temperature

2 pH

3 concentration of the enzyme phosphatase

How the marks are earned

Each correct factor that would change the colour if it varied earns one mark: enzyme concentration, inhibitor concentration, temperature, pH, or using the same colorimeter filter. Substrate concentration is the independent variable and earns nothing.
