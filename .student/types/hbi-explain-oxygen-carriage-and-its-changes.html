Explain red-cell adaptations and the effects of more or less oxygen carriage

How red-cell structure suits oxygen transport, and what follows when haemoglobin, red-cell number or oxygenation changes.

Type 12 Internal transport HBI-EXPLAIN-OXYGEN-CARRIAGE-AND-ITS-CHANGES

★★★☆☆ · 5 of 36 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Medium.

Several linked steps from a change to its effect on the whole body. Needs the link between oxygen, aerobic respiration and energy, which is standard content. No calculation; reading a data table at most. Contexts such as sickle cells, haemolytic anaemia, altitude training and a hole in the septum are new to students. Knowledge card

Explain red-cell adaptations and the effects of more or less oxygen carriage

Written
  • Haemoglobin is a protein in red cells. It combines with oxygen to form oxyhaemoglobin in the lungs and lets go of oxygen in the tissues.
  • Red cells have no nucleus, so there is more space for haemoglobin.
  • The biconcave shape gives a large surface area for oxygen to diffuse in and out.
  • Red cells are small and flexible, so they can squeeze through narrow capillaries; their thin membrane keeps the diffusion distance short.
  • Less oxygen carried means less oxygen reaches respiring cells, so less aerobic respiration and less energy is released. The person feels tired, and breathing and heart rate rise.
  • More haemoglobin or more red cells (for example after altitude training) means more oxygen reaches muscles, more aerobic respiration and more energy.
  • In sickle cell anaemia the haemoglobin is a different form. In low oxygen the red cells become sickle-shaped, with less surface area, and they can block capillaries, so less oxygen is carried.
  • A hole in the septum lets deoxygenated blood from the right side mix with oxygenated blood in the left side. Blood going to the body carries less oxygen, so the person breathes faster to take in more oxygen.

Before you begin

  • Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and glucose to release energy.
  • Diffusion is faster with a larger surface area and a shorter distance.
  • Structure of capillaries and alveoli.
1

The method

  1. For a structure question, write each feature and then 'so' and its benefit.

    Why: Each mark needs the feature linked to its advantage.

  2. For a change question, start with the change itself (less haemoglobin, fewer red cells, mixed blood).

    Why: The first scheme point is usually the cause stated in your own words.

  3. Follow the chain: oxygen carried, oxygen reaching cells or muscles, aerobic respiration, energy.

    Why: Marks build up along this chain, one per link.

  4. End with the result the question asks for: tiredness, faster breathing, higher heart rate or better performance.

    Why: The final link connects your chain to the question.

  5. Use comparative words such as 'less' or 'more' in every link.

    Why: A link without a direction does not explain the change.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Saying red cells have no nucleus 'so they can carry more oxygen' without mentioning more haemoglobin.
  • ✗
    Writing that respiration 'makes energy'; energy is released, not made.
  • ✗
    Stopping the chain at 'less oxygen' and not reaching respiration and energy.
  • ✗
    For a hole in the heart, saying the blood has 'no oxygen' instead of less oxygen because deoxygenated blood mixes with oxygenated blood.
  • ✗
    Describing the biconcave shape without saying it increases surface area for diffusion.

Worked explanation

  1. The question asks how the structure of a red cell suits its job. Its job is to carry oxygen. There are 4 marks.
  2. Feature 1: it contains haemoglobin. Benefit: haemoglobin joins with oxygen.
  3. Feature 2: it has no nucleus. Benefit: there is more room for haemoglobin, so more oxygen can be carried.
  4. Feature 3: it is biconcave. Benefit: a large surface area for oxygen to diffuse.
  5. Feature 4: it is small and flexible. Benefit: it can pass through narrow capillaries.
  6. Feature 5: thin membrane. Benefit: oxygen has only a short way to diffuse. I give five pairs to be safe.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

The red cell contains haemoglobin, which combines with oxygen so it can be carried. It has no nucleus, so there is more space for haemoglobin. Its biconcave shape gives a large surface area for oxygen to diffuse in and out. It is small and flexible, so it can squeeze through narrow capillaries. Its membrane is thin, so the diffusion distance for oxygen is short.

How the marks are earned

Each feature joined to its benefit earns a mark: haemoglobin joining with oxygen; no nucleus giving room for more haemoglobin; biconcave shape raising surface area; small or flexible size for moving through capillaries; thin membrane shortening diffusion. Any four give full marks. A feature with no benefit is weaker.
