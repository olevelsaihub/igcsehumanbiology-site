Idea. Immunity means your body can destroy a pathogen before you become ill. It has two names. Active or passive: who made the antibodies? Natural or artificial: did it happen in normal life, or from a medical procedure? Natural active: you catch the disease. Artificial active: you have a vaccine. Natural passive: a baby gets its mother's antibodies across the placenta, or in breast milk. Artificial passive: an injection of antibodies, or plasma from someone who has recovered.

Method. Step one: ask who made the antibodies. If the person's own lymphocytes, the white blood cells that make antibodies, made them, it is active. It is slow to start, but memory cells make it last. Memory cells remember the antigen for years. If the antibodies were received ready-made, it is passive. It works at once, but no memory cells are made, so it does not last. Step two: ask how it happened. In normal life, it is natural. From a medical procedure, such as an injection, it is artificial. A vaccine is artificial and active. On a graph, passive immunity is high at once, then falls quickly as the antibodies are broken down. Active immunity rises slowly, then stays high for longer. Someone already ill needs antibodies now, so give passive immunity. Plasma from a recovered person has antibodies that bind to the virus antigens, so phagocytes destroy it. The plasma is tested first, and the red cells are removed so the blood does not clump.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Two graphs show antibody levels after two types of injection. We will draw them next. Identify the form of artificial immunity each injection gives. Two marks. Both are injections, so both are artificial. Injection one: the level is high almost at once, and it falls to nothing by about forty days. Ready-made antibodies were injected. So it is artificial passive. Injection two: the level rises slowly to a peak at about thirty days, and stays up much longer. The body made its own antibodies. So it is artificial active. Common mistake. A common mistake is to call a vaccine passive, because the person does nothing. A vaccine makes the body produce its own antibodies, so it is active. Another mistake is to call antibodies in breast milk artificial. Breast feeding is normal life, so it is natural passive. And passive immunity never lasts long.

Your turn. Classify each one. One: a farmer catches measles from her child and recovers. Two: a man bitten by a snake is given antibodies against the venom. Three: a nurse has a hepatitis B vaccine. Then say which one works fastest. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. The farmer caught measles, so her own lymphocytes made antibodies, in normal life: natural active. The nurse's vaccine is artificial active. The snakebite antibodies were injected ready-made: artificial passive. The snakebite injection works fastest. The antibodies are ready-made, so they act at once. The other two need the body to make its own antibodies, which takes days. So, every time: who made the antibodies decides active or passive. How it happened decides natural or artificial.
