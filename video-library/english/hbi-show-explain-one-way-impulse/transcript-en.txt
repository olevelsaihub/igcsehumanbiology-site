Idea. A nerve impulse is an electrical signal that travels along a neurone. A neurone is a nerve cell. Two neurones meet at a synapse. A synapse is a junction with a tiny gap between the neurones. An impulse can cross a synapse in one way only: from the first neurone to the next one. It never goes backwards. This lesson shows why, and how to draw it.

Method. Reason one. Vesicles are tiny sacs that hold a chemical called a neurotransmitter. Vesicles are only in the synaptic knob, the swollen end of the first neurone. So the neurotransmitter is released from that side only. Reason two. The neurotransmitter diffuses across the gap. Diffuse means spread from a high concentration to a low concentration. Concentration means how much is packed into a space. It is high next to the knob and low by the next neurone, so it spreads one way. Reason three. Receptors are protein molecules that the neurotransmitter fits into. They are only on the membrane of the next neurone, the post-synaptic membrane. So a new impulse can only start there. So here are the three reasons in order. It is released from one side. It diffuses across. Its receptors are on the other side. Which structure keeps impulses one way? The synapse. Now a reflex arc, a fast automatic response. The receptor, which detects a change, sends impulses along the sensory neurone, then the relay neurone, then the motor neurone, to the effector. An effector is a muscle or gland that responds. There are synapses between the neurones. So you draw the arrows from sensory to motor. To mark the receptor, find the free end of the sensory neurone. It is the end farthest from the spinal cord, out in the skin. The receptor joins there.

Worked example. The diagram shows a synapse. The fibres on the left end in synaptic knobs on the next neurone. So here, impulses travel from left to right. Part three asks why an impulse can only travel in one direction across a synapse. Four marks. Point one: the vesicles are only in the synaptic knob, so neurotransmitter can only be released from the first neurone. Point two: it diffuses across the gap. Point three: it diffuses from a high concentration near the knob to a low concentration. Point four: the receptors are only on the post-synaptic membrane of the second neurone. Common mistake. When you draw, never draw two arrows pointing opposite ways to be safe. That scores zero. Never draw the arrow from the next neurone back to the knob. Draw one arrow, from the knob side across the gap. Do not write that the impulse jumps the gap. The chemical crosses. And the structure that keeps impulses one way is the synapse, not the relay neurone or the spinal cord.

Your turn. In a reflex arc, a scientist stimulates the middle of a relay neurone with a tiny electrode. Impulses travel both ways along the relay neurone from that point. Explain why the motor neurone gets an impulse, but the sensory neurone does not. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. From the electrode, one impulse goes towards the motor neurone. It reaches the relay knob. Vesicles release neurotransmitter, it diffuses across, and binds to receptors on the motor neurone. The other impulse goes towards the sensory neurone. That end has no vesicles to release neurotransmitter, and the sensory knob has no receptors. So the impulse stops. So, every time: draw one arrow from the knob side. Explain with three ideas: it is released from one side, diffuses high to low, and its receptors are on the other side. Name the synapse as the one-way structure.
