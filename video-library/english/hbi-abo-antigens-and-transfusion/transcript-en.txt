Red blood cells carry markers on their surface, called antigens. An antigen is a molecule the body can recognise. Group A cells carry antigen A. The plasma carries antibodies. An antibody is a protein that sticks to one kind of antigen. Group A plasma has anti-B antibodies. If an antibody meets the antigen it matches, it sticks to it. Each antibody can hold two cells, so the red cells clump together. This clumping is called agglutination. The clumps can block blood vessels, and the patient can die.

Step one: make a mini table. Group A has antigen A and anti-B antibodies. Group B has antigen B and anti-A antibodies. Group AB has both antigens, and neither antibody. Group O has no antigens, but both antibodies, anti-A and anti-B. Step two: check the donor's antigens against the recipient's antibodies. A transfusion means giving blood from one person, the donor, to another person, the recipient. Group B blood into a group A person: her anti-B sticks to antigen B, so the cells clump. Not safe. So group O blood can be given to every group, because it has no antigens. And a group AB person can receive every group, because they have no anti-A or anti-B antibodies. For a calculation, first list the groups that can safely receive the blood. Add their percentages. Then multiply by the total number of people, and divide by one hundred.

Here is a real exam question. There are four blood groups, A, B, AB and O. Explain which group, or groups, can be given to people with each blood group. It is worth four marks. Recipient A has anti-B, so no blood with antigen B. Safe: A and O. Recipient B has anti-A, so no antigen A. Safe: B and O. Recipient AB has no anti-A or anti-B antibodies, so all four groups are safe. Recipient O has both antibodies, so only group O is safe. Here is the full answer, one line for each group. A common mistake is to say group O has antigen O. There is no antigen O. Group O red cells have no A or B antigens. Another mistake is to say the wrong blood makes the blood clot. The right word is agglutination: the red cells clump together.

Your turn. In a town of two thousand people, forty per cent are group A, ten per cent group B, five per cent group AB and forty-five per cent group O. How many people could safely receive group A blood? Pause here and work it out.

Group A blood carries antigen A. So it is safe only for people with no anti-A antibodies. That is group A and group AB. Add their percentages: forty plus five is forty-five per cent. Then forty-five times two thousand, divided by one hundred, is nine hundred people. Every time: make the mini table. Check the donor's antigens against the recipient's antibodies. If they match, the cells clump. If not, the blood is safe.
