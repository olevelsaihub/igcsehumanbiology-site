Blood enters the kidney filter. It flows into the glomerulus, a knot of tiny blood vessels. The knot sits in a cup, the Bowman's capsule. Then the blood leaves. The filter works like a sieve. Small molecules can pass through the holes. Large ones cannot, so they stay behind.

Step one: pressure. The vessel bringing blood in is wider. The vessel taking blood out is narrower. Blood comes in faster than it can leave, so there is a high pressure in the glomerulus. Step two: this pressure forces small molecules out of the blood, into the capsule. They are water, glucose, urea, salts and amino acids. This is ultrafiltration: filtering under high pressure. Step three: blood cells and proteins are too large to pass. They stay in the blood. So if protein is found in urine, the glomerulus must be damaged. Step four: the filtered liquid is called the filtrate. It flows on into the nephron, a long, thin kidney tube. In the proximal convoluted tubule, all the glucose is taken back into the blood. This is selective reabsorption. In untreated diabetes, blood glucose is very high. Normally, the tubule takes back all the glucose. But now there is too much in the filtrate. Not all of it is taken back, so some is left in the urine.

Now a real exam question. The diagram shows a Bowman's capsule with two vessels, B, and A, on the left. Explain how vessel B, and vessel A, help ultrafiltration. Four marks. Vessel A, on the left, brings blood into the glomerulus. Vessel B takes blood away. In the diagram, vessel B is a little narrower than vessel A. Blood enters faster than it can leave. So the blood pressure in the glomerulus rises. This high pressure forces small molecules out of the blood, into the capsule. Here is the full answer. A common mistake is to write only "pressure". Say why it is high: the vessel leaving is narrower than the vessel entering. Another mistake is to say protein is filtered and then taken back. Protein is never filtered. It is too large to pass.

Your turn. A drug makes the vessel that brings blood into the glomerulus narrower. Suggest how this affects ultrafiltration, and what happens to urea in the blood. Pause here and work it out.

Now the vessel in is narrower too, so less blood comes in. The pressure in the glomerulus is lower. Lower pressure forces fewer small molecules out, so less filtrate is made. Less urea is removed, so urea builds up in the blood. So remember: narrow vessel out gives high pressure. Pressure forces small molecules out. Large cells and proteins stay. All glucose is taken back in the proximal tubule.
