Idea. The small intestine is where digested food is absorbed into the blood. Its lining is folded into millions of tiny fingers, called villi. One of them is called a villus. Small food molecules, such as glucose, move into the blood by diffusion. Diffusion is movement from where there is a lot, to where there is less. Each feature of a villus makes this faster.

Method. Feature one: there are very many villi, and the cells on each villus have tiny folds called microvilli. So the surface area for absorption is huge. Feature two: the wall of the villus is only one cell thick. So glucose has only a short distance to diffuse. Feature three: each villus has a dense network of capillaries, the smallest blood vessels. Blood flows through and carries the glucose away. This keeps the glucose concentration in the blood low. So the difference in concentration between the gut and the blood stays big: we call this a steep concentration gradient. Feature four: in the middle is a lacteal. It is a lymph vessel. It absorbs fatty acids and glycerol, the products of fat digestion. Now damage. In coeliac disease, the villi are flattened. The surface area falls, so less is absorbed. Less calcium is absorbed, so calcium is taken from the bones, and they become weak. Gut worms also take nutrients, so fewer reach the blood.

Worked example. Now a real exam question. The diagram shows a structure from the small intestine: a villus. Explain how these structures increase the rate of diffusion of glucose into the blood. Three marks. Point one: there are many villi, with microvilli, so there is a large surface area. Point two: the wall is one cell thick, so the diffusion distance is short. Point three: many capillaries carry the glucose away in the blood, so a steep concentration gradient is kept. Here is the full answer: three features, each with its effect. Common mistake. A common mistake is to say villi have thin cell walls. Human cells have no cell wall. Say the wall of the villus is one cell thick. Another mistake is to list features with no effect, or to say villi digest food. Villi absorb. Give each feature, then what it does for absorption.

Your turn. After an operation, a man has a much shorter small intestine. He eats normally, but he loses weight. Explain why. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. A shorter small intestine has fewer villi. So there is a smaller surface area for absorption. So less glucose and other food is absorbed into his blood. His cells get less energy from food, so the body uses its stores, and he loses weight. So, every time: pick a feature of the villus, and link it to its effect. For damage: flattened or fewer villi, less surface area, less absorbed, then the result.
