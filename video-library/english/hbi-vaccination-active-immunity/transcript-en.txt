Idea. A vaccine contains antigens from a pathogen. A pathogen is a microorganism that causes disease, and an antigen is a protein on its surface. The vaccine may hold a dead or weakened pathogen, or just its antigens. It cannot make you ill. Lymphocytes, a type of white blood cell, detect the antigens. They make antibodies that fit those antigens. Some lymphocytes become memory cells, which stay in the body for years. Active immunity means your own body makes the antibodies. Catching the disease itself does the same: that is natural active immunity.

Method. Here is the antibody level after a vaccine. The first response is slow and small. But memory cells are made, and they stay. Later the real pathogen enters. Now memory cells make large amounts of antibodies very quickly. The level passes the line before symptoms can start. So the pathogen is destroyed and the person does not become ill. Antibody levels fall over time. Here, they drop below the safe level. A booster dose of the vaccine raises them again, and makes more memory cells. When most people are vaccinated, few people catch and carry the pathogen. So it spreads less. Even people who are not vaccinated are protected. This is herd immunity.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. Explain how vaccination can give a person immunity to a disease. Five marks. Step one: a dead or weakened form of the pathogen is injected. It carries antigens. Step two: lymphocytes detect the antigens and make antibodies. Step three: some antibodies stay in the blood for a while. Step four: memory cells are made, and they stay in the body. Step five: if the real pathogen enters, memory cells quickly make many antibodies, so the person does not become ill. Answer by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme. So the full answer has five linked steps, in order, from the injection to the fast response.

Common mistake. A common mistake is to say the vaccine contains antibodies. It contains antigens. Your own lymphocytes make the antibodies. Another mistake is to write 'the body remembers the disease'. Name the memory cells, and say they make antibodies faster next time.

Your turn. Leena had chickenpox when she was five, and she was ill. At twenty, she meets the chickenpox virus again, but she does not become ill. Explain why. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. At five, her lymphocytes detected the virus antigens and made antibodies. This was slow, so she was ill. But memory cells were made, and they stayed. At twenty, her memory cells recognised the antigens. They made large amounts of antibodies very quickly. The virus was destroyed before symptoms began. So, every time: antigens in, lymphocytes make antibodies, memory cells stay, and the next response is fast and large.
