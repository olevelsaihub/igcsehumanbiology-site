Enzymes are biological catalysts. They speed up reactions in the body without being used up. Each enzyme works fastest at one temperature, called the optimum. For human enzymes, it is about thirty-seven degrees Celsius. Below the optimum, warming makes the rate rise. Above it, the rate falls fast, because the enzyme is denatured. Denatured means the shape of its active site has changed for good.

Step one: decide which side of the optimum you are on. Below it, warming gives the molecules more kinetic energy. Kinetic energy is energy of movement. So they move faster and collide more often, and more enzyme-substrate complexes form each second. The rate rises. Above the optimum, the enzyme is denatured. The active site changes shape, so the substrate cannot fit, and fewer complexes form. At low temperature the rate is slow, but the enzyme is not denatured. Each enzyme also has an optimum pH. This made-up graph shows two enzymes. Pepsin, in the acid stomach, works best at about pH 2. Lipase works best in slightly alkaline conditions. A pH far from the optimum changes the active site, and denatures the enzyme. Gut enzyme tests are kept at thirty-seven degrees Celsius. That is body temperature, near the optimum, so the enzyme works fast and is not denatured. If the body gets too cold, enzymes slow down, metabolism slows, and the heart rate falls.

Now a real exam question. The graph shows the activity of a bacterial enzyme. The question asks: explain what is causing the change in activity from A to B. Five marks. A is at about forty-three degrees, and B at about fifty-eight degrees. The activity rises from A to B. So this is the rising side, before the optimum. Point one: the temperature rises. Point two: the molecules gain more kinetic energy. Point three: they collide more often. Point four: more enzyme-substrate complexes form each second. A common mistake is to say the enzyme is killed. Enzymes are proteins, not living things. They are denatured. Another mistake is to say low temperature denatures enzymes. Cold only slows them down, because there are fewer collisions.

Your turn. Pepsin digests protein in the stomach, at about pH 2. When food moves into the small intestine, the pH is about 8. Explain why pepsin stops working there. Pause here and work it out.

Pepsin's optimum pH is about 2. pH 8 is far from its optimum, so its activity falls to nothing. At pH 8, pepsin is denatured. Its active site changes shape, so protein can no longer bind. No enzyme-substrate complexes form, so protein is not digested. So, every time: find which side of the optimum you are on. Below it: kinetic energy, collisions, complexes, rate. Too hot or the wrong pH: denatured, active site changed, fewer complexes.
