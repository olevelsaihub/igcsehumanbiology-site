Idea. In an investigation, you change one factor and measure its effect. Everything else must stay the same. Then you repeat, find a mean, and compare. A mean is the average: add the results and divide by how many there are. Pulse rate is the number of times the heart beats in one minute. To measure it, put two fingers on the artery in the wrist, or the neck. An artery is a blood vessel that carries blood away from the heart. Count the beats for fifteen seconds, then multiply by four to get beats per minute. A pulse monitor also works.

Method. Step one: take a resting value first. This is your starting point, called a baseline. Without it you cannot see a change. Step two: say what you change, and give its values. For example, exercise for twenty, then forty seconds. Step three: say exactly how you measure the effect, and when: pulse taken straight after stopping. Before the next go, rest until the pulse is back to its resting value. Then do the next exercise time. Step five: name at least two control variables, the things you keep the same: the same person, the same exercise, at the same speed. Step six: repeat each test, and calculate a mean. The same plan works for other tests on people. Reaction time: a dropped ruler, the distance it falls. Sweating: absorbent paper on the skin, weighed before and after. Catching: count catches with both eyes, then one eye covered. Hearing: raise the sound frequency, how high the sound is, until it cannot be heard, in many people of each age. Urine: collect all of it for a set time, on a hot day and a cold day, and measure the volume. For a drug or diet trial, use two groups matched for age, sex and health. Only one group gets the treatment. The other gets nothing, or a placebo: a dummy pill with no drug in it. After a set time, measure both and compare.

Worked example. Now a real exam question. A student investigates the effect of time spent exercising on pulse rate. The graph shows pulse rate for exercise times from zero to one hundred seconds. The question asks how the student could carry out the investigation. Six marks. First, sit still for five minutes, then take the resting pulse: two fingers on the wrist, count for fifteen seconds, times four. Then do step-ups for twenty seconds, and take the pulse straight after. Rest until the pulse is back to resting. Repeat with forty, sixty, eighty and one hundred seconds. Same person, same exercise, same speed. Do each time three times and find the mean. This answer is by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is to forget the resting pulse, or to write measure the pulse without saying how. Another is starting the next exercise before the pulse is back to normal. In a drug trial, do not give everyone the drug. You need a group without it to compare. And for sweating, say how you measure the sweat, not just the amount of sweat.

Your turn. Plan an investigation to find out how light intensity, meaning the brightness of light, affects the size of a person's pupil. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. Start in a dim room. Change the light intensity, with lamps of different brightness. Wait for the pupil to adjust, photograph the eye, and measure the pupil diameter. Keep the same person and the same distance from the lamp. Repeat each one, and find a mean. So, every time: a baseline, what you change and its values, how you measure, two controls, then repeats and a mean.
