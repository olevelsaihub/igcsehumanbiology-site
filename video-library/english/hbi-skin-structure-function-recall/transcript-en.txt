Your skin has three main layers. The epidermis is the thin outer layer. Below it is the thick dermis. Under the dermis is a layer of fat, also called adipose tissue. The skin is a barrier that keeps pathogens out. Pathogens are microbes that cause disease. The epidermis also holds melanin, a brown pigment. It absorbs ultraviolet light from the sun, so it lowers the risk of skin cancer.

Step one: find the layer, then name the structure exactly. In the dermis, each hair grows from a hair follicle, a small pit. Beside it is a sebaceous gland. It makes an oily liquid that keeps the hair and skin soft. The sweat gland makes sweat. It flows up a tube to a pore, a tiny hole in the surface. Sweat cools you and excretes, or removes, some water and salts. The dermis also has blood vessels, and receptors that sense touch, pain and temperature. Step two: give a specific job, not a vague one. The fat layer is an insulator: it reduces the loss of heat from the body. Fat is a poor conductor, which means heat moves through it slowly. Step three, for burns: a first degree burn damages only the epidermis. A second degree burn reaches the dermis. A third degree burn also reaches the fat. If skin is burned or cut, pathogens can get through into the body and the blood. Step four: several structures help control body temperature. The hypothalamus, a part of the brain, detects the blood temperature. Then sweat glands, the arterioles in the skin, and the muscles respond. Arterioles are small vessels that carry blood into the skin.

Fat is a poor conductor of heat. So point one: the fat layer is an insulator. Point two: it reduces the heat energy lost from the body to the surroundings. A common mistake is to write that fat keeps the body warm. That is too vague, and it does not score. Say it insulates, and reduces heat loss. Also, do not call the fat layer muscle. Another mistake is to mix up sweat glands and sebaceous glands. And do not write that melanin blocks the sun. Melanin absorbs ultraviolet light.
