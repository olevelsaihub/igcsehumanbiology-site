A correlation means two things change together. On hot days, more ice cream is sold, and more people get sunburn. They rise together. But ice cream does not cause sunburn. Hot weather causes both. So a correlation does not prove a cause. Good evidence also needs good data. A sample is the group of people tested. A small sample may not be reliable. Other factors, like age, diet or exercise, can change the result. And body readings go up and down, so one reading is not enough.

Here is the method. Step one: read the claim. Underline exactly what it says will happen. Every mark is about that claim. Step two: find the data that test the claim. Quote one or two real figures, with their units. Your opinion earns nothing. The numbers do. Step three: say what the data cannot show. Name the problem. Is the sample small? Could other factors change the result? Do the values vary? Is a time point missing? If every reading is already past the healthy limit, the data cannot show when the problem began. And if values go up and down, a prediction from the trend is uncertain. Step four: give a judgement. The data support the claim, partly support it, or do not support it. A discuss question always needs this. Sometimes you must find an anomalous result. An anomalous result is one value that does not fit the pattern of the others. In this made-up example, the values rise steadily, but reading four is far too low. Name the value, and say it does not fit.

A common mistake: two lines rise together, so you write that one causes the other. A link is not proof of cause. Another mistake is to write only that the results are not reliable. Always say why. For example, too few people were tested.

Your turn. A company says its new drink lowers blood glucose. Four people drink it for four weeks. Their mean blood glucose falls from six point zero to five point six. They also started to exercise more. Discuss whether the data support the claim. Pause here and work it out.

First, the evidence. The mean, or average, blood glucose falls by zero point four, from six point zero to five point six. This agrees with the claim. Now the limits. Only four people were tested. And they also exercised more, and exercise lowers blood glucose too. So the data only partly support the claim. So, every time: read the claim. Quote the data. Name what the data cannot show. Then give your judgement.
