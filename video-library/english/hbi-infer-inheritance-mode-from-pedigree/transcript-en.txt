A pedigree can tell you how a condition is inherited. An allele is one form of a gene. Ask two questions. First: dominant or recessive? Look for two unaffected parents with an affected child. The allele was hidden in both parents, so it is recessive. Both parents are carriers: each has the allele but not the condition. Now the opposite. Two affected parents have an unaffected child. Each parent had a hidden recessive allele and passed it on. So the condition is dominant: one allele is enough for it to show, and both parents are capital H, small h. With a dominant condition, every affected person has an affected parent.

Second question: is it on the X? X-linked means the allele is on the X chromosome. A male has only one X, so one recessive allele shows in him. Count affected males and females. An X-linked recessive condition affects many more males. Affected sons often have unaffected mothers, who are carriers. Now test it. Find an affected daughter. If the condition were X-linked recessive, she needs a faulty X from her father. His only X would carry it, so he would be affected too. An unaffected father means it is not X-linked. A second test: an affected father with an affected son. A father gives his Y to his sons, not his X. So an X-linked allele cannot pass from father to son. If it passes only from father to son, it is Y-linked. Some questions ask you to draw the pedigree. Join partners with one line. Draw a line down to their children. Put each generation on its own row. Shade the affected people, and always add a key.

Here is a real exam question. Alkaptonuria is caused by a recessive allele. Use the family pedigree to explain whether the condition is sex-linked. Four marks. First, count. Two females are affected, persons 6 and 8. Only one male is affected, person 13. An X-linked recessive condition would affect more males, not more females. Now test the daughters. Person 6 is affected, but her father, person 1, is not. Person 8 is affected, but her father, person 3, is not. If it were sex-linked, each father would pass his X to his daughter, so he would be affected too. So males 1 and 3 must be carriers. But under sex linkage, males cannot be carriers. So alkaptonuria is not sex-linked. Explanation by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme.

A common mistake is to say a condition is sex-linked just because a male is affected. Males and females can both have most conditions. Use the tests. Another mistake is to give a conclusion with no evidence. Always name the people, by number, and say what they show.

Your turn. Ali and Sara do not have a condition. Their son Omar has it. Their daughter Hina does not. Hina and Bilal do not have it, but their daughter Zara does. Draw the pedigree. Is it dominant or recessive? Is it X-linked? Pause here and work it out.

Draw Ali and Sara joined by a line, with Omar and Hina below. Shade Omar. Join Hina to Bilal, and draw Zara below them, shaded. Add the key. It is recessive: Ali and Sara are unaffected, but their son Omar is affected. It is not X-linked: Zara is affected, but her father Bilal is not. So, every time: unaffected parents with an affected child means recessive. Then test the affected daughters and their fathers. Name the people.
