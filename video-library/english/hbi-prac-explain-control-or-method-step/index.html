Idea. A control is a copy of the test, with the factor being tested left out. Here, amylase digests starch. Amylase is an enzyme, a protein that speeds up a reaction. The test tube has starch and amylase. The control tube has starch and water instead of amylase. Everything else is the same. After ten minutes, add iodine. Iodine turns blue-black when starch is there. The test goes orange-brown, so the starch is gone. The control is blue-black, so the starch is still there. So the amylase caused the change.

Method. Step one. To describe a control, copy the test and leave out only the tested factor. Replace the enzyme with the same volume of water, or of boiled enzyme. Boiling denatures an enzyme: its shape changes, so it stops working. The total volume stays the same. For three marks, give three ideas. First, the set-up. Second, the expected result: in the control, nothing changes. Third, what it shows: the tested factor caused the change in the test. Some questions ask why a step is in a method. Name the error it prevents, and how it changes the reading. Potato pieces are blotted dry before they are weighed. Liquid left on the surface adds extra mass, so the reading would be too high. Another common step is using percentage change in mass. Pieces often start with different masses. This piece gains 0.2 grams. This bigger one gains 0.4 grams. But both changes are 10 per cent. So percentage change lets you compare pieces fairly.

Worked example. Here is a real exam question. A student tests whether antibiotic X works better than antibiotic Y. Each one is soaked into a disc of filter paper, on bacteria growing on agar. Agar is a jelly that bacteria grow on. The question asks you to explain a suitable control. An antibiotic is a drug that kills bacteria or stops them growing. Bacteria cover the agar. Around each antibiotic disc there is a clear zone, where no bacteria grow. The factor being tested is the antibiotic. Point one is the set-up. Add a third disc of filter paper, soaked in sterile water, with no antibiotic. Sterile means free of microbes. Point two is the expected result. Bacteria grow right up to this disc, so there is no clear zone. Point three is what the control shows. The clear zones are caused by the antibiotics, not by the filter paper. Here is the full answer. Common mistake. A common mistake is to describe a control as keeping everything the same. That is about control variables, not a control experiment. Say what is left out, and what replaces it. Another mistake is saying a step is there to make it fair. Name the error instead. Surface water adds to the mass, so the reading is too high.

Your turn. A student mixes milk with the enzyme lipase. Lipase digests fat into fatty acids, which lower the pH. A lower pH means more acidic. She measures the pH every minute. Explain a suitable control for her investigation. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. Set-up: the same volume of milk, with 2 cubic centimetres of boiled lipase instead of active lipase. The same volume of water instead of lipase would also work. Expected result: in the control, the pH stays the same. This shows that the lipase caused the pH to fall in the test, and not the milk on its own. So, every time: copy the test, and leave out the tested factor. Say the expected result. Then say what it shows. For a method step, name the error it prevents.
