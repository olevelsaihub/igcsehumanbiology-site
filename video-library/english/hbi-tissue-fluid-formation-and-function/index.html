Your body cells do not touch the blood. They are bathed in a liquid called tissue fluid. It fills the small spaces between the cells, close to the capillaries. Tissue fluid is the go-between. It brings oxygen and glucose from the blood to the cells. It takes carbon dioxide and other wastes away from the cells.

Here is a capillary bed, a network of capillaries. An arteriole is a small artery, and a venule is a small vein. Blood comes in at the arteriole end and leaves at the venule end. The capillary wall has small gaps, called pores. At the arteriole end, the blood pressure is high. It forces water and small molecules out through the pores: glucose, amino acids, oxygen and ions. This liquid is tissue fluid. Red blood cells and plasma proteins, the large proteins dissolved in the plasma, are too big to pass through the pores. So they stay in the blood. Along the capillary the blood pressure falls. At the venule end it is lower than the pull of the plasma proteins. So some water is drawn back into the blood. The extra tissue fluid drains into lymph vessels. Inside them, it is called lymph. When muscles contract, they squeeze the lymph vessels and move the lymph along. If the fluid cannot drain, it builds up and the tissue swells. Some questions give two pressures. Take the protein pull away from the blood pressure. If the answer is positive, fluid goes out. If it is negative, fluid comes back in.

Here is a real exam question. Body cells are surrounded by tissue fluid. Explain how tissue fluid is formed. It is worth four marks. Point one, where: at the arteriole end of the capillary. Point two, how: the capillary wall has small pores. Point three, why: the blood pressure there is high. Point four, what: it forces water and small molecules, such as glucose, out through the pores. Red cells and large proteins stay behind. Here is the full answer. This explanation is by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme.

A common mistake is to say red blood cells and proteins leave the blood. They are too big to pass through the pores. Another mistake is to say fluid is pushed out at the venule end, or that it forms by diffusion only. It is pushed out by high pressure, at the arteriole end.

Your turn. After a long flight, Omar's ankles are swollen. He sat still for ten hours, so the muscles in his legs hardly contracted. Explain why tissue fluid built up in his ankles. Pause here and work it out.

Tissue fluid keeps forming at the arteriole ends. The extra fluid should drain into lymph vessels. But lymph is moved along when muscles contract and squeeze the vessels. His muscles hardly contracted, so the lymph did not move. The fluid built up, and his ankles swelled. Every time: say where, at the arteriole end. Say why, high pressure. Say how, through pores. Then say what leaves, and what stays.
