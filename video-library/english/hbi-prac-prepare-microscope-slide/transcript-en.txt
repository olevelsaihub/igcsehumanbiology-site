Idea. Human cells are thin, and almost see-through. Under a light microscope, light must pass through them. With no stain, you can hardly see them. A stain is a dye that colours parts of the cell, so they show up clearly.

Method. Step one: collect the cells cleanly. Gently rub a sterile cotton bud on the inside of your cheek. Sterile means it has no microbes on it. Microbes are tiny living things, such as bacteria. Step two: smear the cells thinly on a clean slide. A thin layer lets light pass through one layer of cells. Step three: add a drop of stain, such as methylene blue. Step four: lower a coverslip at an angle, so no air bubbles are trapped. A coverslip is a thin square of glass. Then soak up extra stain with a paper towel. Step five: put the slide on the stage. Start with the low power objective lens, then focus. For blood, prick a finger with a sterile lancet, a small sharp blade. Then let the smear dry before you stain it.

Common mistake. The most common mistake is to forget the stain. Then the cells are almost see-through, and the student sees nothing. Other mistakes: a thick blob instead of a thin smear, a cotton bud that is not sterile, or putting the slide on the stage before the cells are on it.
