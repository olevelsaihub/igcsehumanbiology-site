Read the narration

A neurone is a nerve cell. An impulse travels along it to the end. But there is a small gap before the next one. The impulse cannot jump the gap. So the first neurone releases a chemical, called a neurotransmitter. The gap and the two neurone ends make a synapse. Acetylcholine and dopamine are two neurotransmitters. There are four steps. The neurotransmitter is released. It diffuses across the gap. It binds to receptors. A receptor is a site on the next neurone that the neurotransmitter fits. Binding starts a new impulse. Then an enzyme breaks it down.

For any drug or disease, ask four questions. Which step does it change? Does it give more or less neurotransmitter on the receptors? So are there more, fewer or no impulses? Then, what happens in the body? In Alzheimer's disease, plaques of a protein build up between neurones. They fill the gap and cover the receptors. So acetylcholine cannot cross or bind. Fewer impulses pass, and memory gets worse. Neurones also die. In Parkinson's disease, the neurones that make dopamine are destroyed. Less dopamine means fewer impulses to the muscles, so movement is shaky, stiff and slow. Too much dopamine does the opposite. It over-stimulates neurones, causing anxiety or a fast heart rate. Alcohol is a depressant: it slows the nervous system. Less neurotransmitter is released and less binds. So fewer impulses pass, and reactions are slower. A driver who has drunk alcohol brakes later.

Now a real exam question about the gap between two neurones. Curare is a poison. It blocks the receptors on the second neurone, and this stops breathing. Acetylcholinesterase is the enzyme in the gap that breaks down acetylcholine. Suggest why acetylcholinesterase inhibitors can reverse curare poisoning. Two marks. First, the problem. The curare sits on the receptors. Acetylcholine cannot bind, so there is no impulse to the breathing muscles. Idea one. An inhibitor stops the enzyme working. So acetylcholine is not broken down, and more of it builds up in the gap. Idea two. Curare and acetylcholine fit the same receptors. With much more acetylcholine, it can compete with curare and bind. Then impulses reach the breathing muscles again. Here is the full answer. Explanation by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme.

A common mistake is to say the inhibitor breaks down the curare, or kills the disease. It does neither. It stops the breakdown of acetylcholine. Another mistake is getting the direction wrong. Less neurotransmitter gives fewer impulses, not more. And alcohol slows things down. It never speeds them up.

Your turn. A black widow spider bite makes the neurones that supply muscles release huge amounts of acetylcholine. Suggest how this affects the muscles. Pause here and work it out.

The venom changes the release step. Much more acetylcholine is released into the gap. More acetylcholine diffuses across and binds to the receptors. So many more impulses pass to the muscles. The muscles contract too much, which gives painful cramps. So every time: which step changes, more or less neurotransmitter, more or fewer impulses, and the effect on the body.
