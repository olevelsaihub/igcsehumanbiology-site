Read the narration

The air you breathe in is called inhaled air. The air you breathe out is called exhaled air. Inhaled air has about zero point zero four per cent carbon dioxide. Exhaled air has about four per cent. That is about a hundred times more. Where does the extra carbon dioxide come from? Body cells make it in respiration, the reactions that release energy from glucose. It goes into the blood, then moves into the air in the lungs. To show it, use limewater. Limewater turns cloudy with carbon dioxide. An indicator is a liquid that changes colour. Hydrogencarbonate indicator turns from red to yellow.

Now the apparatus. Start with one test tube of limewater. A rubber bung closes the top. Two glass tubes go through the bung. The long tube dips into the limewater. The short tube ends above it, in the air. This is the key to the drawing. Now add a second tube, set up the same way, and join both to one mouthpiece. On the left, the mouthpiece joins the short tube. On the right, it joins the long tube. Breathe in through the mouthpiece. Room air is pulled down the long tube on the left, and bubbles through the limewater. So the left tube tests inhaled air. Breathe out through the mouthpiece. Your breath goes down the long tube on the right, and bubbles through. So the right tube tests exhaled air. Make it fair. Use fresh limewater for each test, and time how long each tube takes to go cloudy. To compare rest with exercise, time it at rest, then exercise and time it again with fresh limewater. A shorter time means more carbon dioxide.

Now a real exam question. The diagram shows one test tube of limewater. Tube A reaches down into the limewater. Tube B ends above it. Explain the difference expected between the results for inhaled and exhaled air. Point one: the result. The limewater goes cloudy more quickly with exhaled air. It takes less time. Point two: this is because exhaled air has more carbon dioxide than inhaled air. Point three: why is there more? The extra carbon dioxide is made by respiration in the body cells. Here is the full answer in three points. This explanation is by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme.

A common drawing mistake is to put both tubes into the liquid. Then nothing bubbles in the right way, and you could suck limewater into your mouth. One tube long, one tube short. Another mistake is to say inhaled air has no carbon dioxide. It has a little, about zero point zero four per cent. And the lungs do not make carbon dioxide. Body cells do, in respiration.

Your turn. Sara breathes out through hydrogencarbonate indicator. At rest, it turns yellow in sixty seconds. After running, with fresh indicator, it takes twenty-five seconds. Explain the difference. Pause here and work it out.

After running, the indicator turns yellow in less time. So her breath after running has more carbon dioxide in it. Why? Running muscles respire faster. They need more energy. So they make more carbon dioxide, and she breathes it out. So, every time: say which air has more carbon dioxide, say it changes the indicator in less time, and give the reason: respiration in body cells.
