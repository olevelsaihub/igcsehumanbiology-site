Sound is a wave of vibrations in the air. Vibrate means move quickly back and forth. Your ear turns these vibrations into nerve impulses, the electrical signals that go to the brain. The ear has three parts. First, the outer ear. Next, the middle ear. It holds air and three tiny bones. Last, the inner ear. It holds fluid, in the cochlea.

Step one. The pinna, the outer ear flap, collects sound waves. It sends them along the ear canal. At the end, the eardrum is a thin membrane. It vibrates when the sound hits it. Step two. The ossicles are the three tiny bones: the hammer, the anvil and the stirrup. They pass the vibrations from the eardrum across the middle ear to the oval window. They also amplify the vibrations. Amplify means make bigger. Step three. The cochlea is a coiled tube full of fluid. The vibrations move the fluid. This stimulates hair cells, which are receptors. They start impulses in the auditory nerve, which carries them to the brain. The Eustachian tube joins the middle ear to the throat. It keeps the air pressure the same on both sides of the eardrum, so the eardrum can vibrate freely. The semicircular canals are for balance. When your head moves, their fluid moves, and impulses go to the cerebellum, the part of the brain that keeps balance. Hearing is lost when any step is blocked. In glue ear, fluid fills the middle ear. The eardrum and ossicles cannot vibrate well, so the hair cells are not stimulated, and fewer impulses reach the brain. Loud noise and ageing damage hair cells. High-pitched sounds are lost first.

Now a real exam question. The diagram shows part of an ear. P, Q and R are three small bones next to the eardrum. Describe the functions of structures P, Q and R. Four marks. P, Q and R are the ossicles. Point one: they pass on vibrations. Point two: the vibrations come from the eardrum. Point three: they carry them across the middle ear. Point four: they pass the vibrations on to the oval window of the cochlea. Point five: they amplify the vibrations, making them bigger. Five ideas for four marks is safe. Here is the full answer. A common mistake is to say the ossicles hear the sound, or that the eardrum sends impulses to the brain. Only vibrations pass through the eardrum and ossicles. Impulses start in the cochlea. Do not say the middle ear is full of fluid. It holds air. Fluid there is glue ear. And do not mix up the cochlea, for hearing, with the semicircular canals, for balance.

Your turn. Ali has a cold, and his Eustachian tubes are blocked. When his plane lands, the air pressure outside his ear rises, but the pressure in his middle ear does not change. Explain why Ali cannot hear well. Four marks. Pause here and work it out.

Here is the answer. The blocked Eustachian tube cannot make the pressure equal. The higher pressure outside pushes the eardrum in. So the eardrum cannot vibrate freely. Fewer vibrations pass through the ossicles to the cochlea, so fewer hair cells are stimulated, and fewer impulses go to the brain. So, every time, follow the sound path: pinna, eardrum, ossicles, cochlea, auditory nerve. For each part, say what it does to the vibrations. For hearing loss, find the blocked step.
