Idea. Food stores chemical energy. When food burns, it releases this energy as heat. If we use the heat to warm some water, the water's temperature rises. The bigger the rise, the more energy the food had. To find the energy, multiply three numbers: the mass of water, four point two, and the temperature rise. Four point two is the energy, in joules, that warms one gram of water by one degree Celsius. One cubic centimetre of water has a mass of one gram. Here is an example. Twenty grams of water warms by ten degrees: that is eight hundred and forty joules. Then divide by the mass of food burnt, here half a gram, to get the energy per gram.

Method. Step one: put a measured volume of water, say twenty cubic centimetres, in the boiling tube. Clamp it to the stand. Record the starting temperature. Step two: weigh the food, and push it onto a needle. Light it in a Bunsen flame. Then hold it under the tube until it has completely burnt. Step three: stir the water, and record the highest temperature. Take away the start temperature to find the rise. Step four: repeat for each food. Use the same mass of food, the same volume of fresh water, and the same distance from the food to the tube. Then compare the rises, or the energy per gram. The result is always lower than the true energy in the food. Heat escapes into the air. Some heats the glass and the needle. Some energy leaves as light. And the food may not burn completely. To improve it, hold the food closer, stir the water, and insulate the tube or add a lid, so less heat escapes. A calorimeter is better still. It burns food in oxygen, is insulated, and passes hot gases through a coil in the water.

Worked example. Now a real exam question. The diagram shows a boiling tube held in a clamp, with a thermometer, and food on a needle below it. Describe how this equipment could be used to compare the energy content of different foods. Six marks. Step one: weigh the food, and use the same mass of each food. Step two: record the starting temperature of the water. Step three: burn the food under the tube. Step four: record the final temperature, and work out the rise. Step five: repeat for each food. Step six: compare the temperature rises. The biggest rise means the most energy. This answer is by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is to write measure the temperature only once. You need a start and a final temperature, to find the rise. Another mistake is to say the energy is destroyed or used up. It is not. It goes into the air, the glass and the needle. And repeating the test does not stop these losses.

Your turn. A student burns one gram of a nut under twenty cubic centimetres of water. The temperature rises by thirty degrees Celsius. Calculate the energy per gram. Then give two reasons why the true value is higher. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. Part a: twenty grams of water, times four point two, times thirty, is two thousand five hundred and twenty joules. The nut was one gram, so that is two thousand five hundred and twenty joules per gram. Part b: heat escaped into the air and warmed the glass, so not all of it reached the water. And the nut may not have burnt completely. So, every time: start temperature, burn a weighed food completely, highest temperature, then the rise. Repeat fairly for each food. Then explain the losses, and how to stop them.
