A biosensor measures the amount of one substance, such as glucose in blood. It uses an enzyme, a protein that speeds up one reaction. The enzyme is immobilised: fixed in place, so it is not washed away and can be used again and again. Each enzyme works on one substance. Glucose oxidase converts glucose into products, including hydrogen peroxide. Urease breaks down urea, a waste in urine, into ammonium ions.

The method: find the target substance and the enzyme on the diagram. Explain the membrane. Explain what the enzyme makes. Then link the product to the reading. Step two: the membrane. A partially permeable membrane lets small molecules, like glucose, through. It stops larger molecules and blood cells. Steps three and four: behind the membrane is the enzyme, glucose oxidase. It makes hydrogen peroxide. The electrode, a small metal sensor, detects it and sends a signal to the display. More glucose gives more product, so a bigger reading. With urease, the sensor would detect ammonium ions instead. A glucose test strip also has an enzyme, fixed on a pad. It reacts with glucose and causes a colour change. Compare it with a chart: more glucose, stronger colour. When comparing samples, use the same temperature.

A common mistake is to say the membrane just filters. Say what passes, the small glucose molecules, and what is stopped, blood cells and large molecules. Another mistake is to say the enzyme detects glucose. The enzyme converts glucose. The electrode detects the product. Last, do not stop at 'makes a product'. Name the product from the diagram, and link the amount: more glucose means a higher reading.

Your turn. A biosensor measures urea in urine. Urea passes a partially permeable membrane and reaches immobilised urease. Ammonium ions then reach a sensor. Part a: explain the function of the urease. Part b: suggest why two samples must be tested at the same temperature. Pause here and work it out.

Part a. Behind the membrane, urease breaks down the urea that passes into ammonium ions. The sensor detects the ammonium ions. More urea gives more ammonium ions, so a higher reading. Part b. Temperature changes how fast an enzyme works. When cooler, urease is slower, makes less product, and gives a lower reading. When warmer, it is faster. At the same temperature, only the urea changes the reading. So, every time: find the target and enzyme. Say what the membrane lets through. Name the product. Link more target to a bigger reading.
