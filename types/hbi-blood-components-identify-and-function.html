Knowledge card

Identify blood components and match them to their features and jobs

Written
  • Blood has four components: red blood cells (erythrocytes), white blood cells, platelets and plasma.
  • Red blood cells are small, biconcave discs with no nucleus. They are full of haemoglobin, which carries oxygen.
  • White blood cells are larger than red cells and have a nucleus. Phagocytes have a lobed nucleus and engulf pathogens. Lymphocytes have a large round nucleus and make antibodies.
  • Platelets are tiny fragments of cells, not whole cells. They have no nucleus and they help the blood to clot.
  • Plasma is the pale yellow liquid part of blood. It is about 90% water and carries dissolved substances such as glucose, urea, carbon dioxide and hormones (chemical messengers). It also carries clotting proteins such as fibrinogen and prothrombin.
  • Only red cells contain haemoglobin; only the white cells have a nucleus; plasma is the only fluid component.
  • Plasma has no cells and is pale yellow; whole blood contains red cells, so it looks red.
  • Under a light microscope, red cells look like flat circles (not 3D shapes). White cells are larger than red cells. Platelets are tiny fragments.
  • Freezing blood damages the cells: ice makes them burst or lose their shape, so they can no longer do their job.

Before you begin

  • Basic cell structure: nucleus, cytoplasm and cell membrane.
  • Pathogens are microorganisms such as bacteria and viruses that cause disease.
  • Enzymes and proteins, such as haemoglobin and antibodies, are made by cells.
1

The method

  1. Look at the size, shape and nucleus of each labelled item in the diagram.

    Why: The nucleus and shape are the clues the examiner drew on purpose; they tell you the component.

  2. Decide: no nucleus and pale centre = red cell; large cell with lobed nucleus = phagocyte; large round nucleus filling the cell = lymphocyte; tiny bits = platelets; the empty space between cells = plasma.

    Why: Each correct name is one mark, so a clear rule stops guessing.

  3. For a tick table, work one row (one feature) at a time and ask which components really show it.

    Why: Tables are marked by row, and one wrong extra tick can lose the whole row.

  4. Use the exact name the specification uses, such as phagocyte, lymphocyte or platelets.

    Why: Vague words like 'blood' or 'cell' do not earn the mark.

  5. When asked for a function, give the job of the named component, not its appearance.

    Why: Function marks need an action such as carries oxygen, engulfs pathogens or helps clotting.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Calling platelets 'cells', or choosing an option that groups platelets with the blood cells.
  • ✗
    Labelling the liquid between the cells as 'water' or 'serum' instead of plasma.
  • ✗
    Putting a 'has a nucleus' tick on red blood cells or platelets.
  • ✗
    Writing 'white blood cell makes antibodies and engulfs' for one cell type; lymphocytes make antibodies, phagocytes engulf.
  • ✗
    Adding extra ticks 'just in case', which can cancel a correct row.
  • ✗
    When checking a student's drawing, missing that the white cells are drawn smaller than, or the same size as, the red cells.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. I read the question: name X, Y and Z. That is three marks, one for each label.
  2. X points to the empty area between the cells. Nothing is drawn there, so it must be the liquid part of the blood. That is plasma.
  3. Y points to a small round cell with a pale dip in the middle and no nucleus. The dip shows a biconcave shape. This is a red blood cell.
  4. Z points to a larger cell with a dark nucleus that has lobes, like a curved sausage. A lobed nucleus means a phagocyte, which is a type of white blood cell.
  5. I also notice small irregular bits in the picture. They are platelets, but no label points to them, so I do not need them.
  6. I write one name on each line.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

X: plasma

Y: red blood cell (erythrocyte)

Z: phagocyte (a white blood cell)

How the marks are earned

One mark for each correct name. X earns its mark only for plasma, the fluid. Y earns its mark for red blood cell. Z earns its mark for white blood cell or the more exact name phagocyte; the scheme also accepts a named type of phagocyte.
