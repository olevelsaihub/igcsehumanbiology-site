Set up and explain the indicator practical comparing carbon dioxide in breath

Two-tube limewater or indicator apparatus with a mouthpiece: drawing and using it, naming the indicator, and explaining results for inspired, expired, rest or exercise air.

★★★☆☆ · 5 of 36 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Medium.

Knowledge card

Set up and explain the indicator practical comparing carbon dioxide in breath

Written
  • Inspired (inhaled) air has about 0.04% carbon dioxide; expired (exhaled) air has about 4%.
  • Expired air has more carbon dioxide because body cells make it in respiration and it diffuses into the alveoli.
  • Limewater turns cloudy (milky) with carbon dioxide. Hydrogencarbonate indicator also changes colour (red to yellow).
  • In each tube, the long tube dips into the indicator and the short tube ends above it. Air is pushed in through the long tube and bubbles through.
  • Breathing out through the long-tube side bubbles expired air through the liquid; breathing in through the short-tube side pulls room air in through the other tube's long tube.
  • Fair comparison: time how long each tube takes to change, or breathe for the same number of breaths, using fresh indicator each time.
  • To compare rest with exercise: at rest, breathe in and out through the mouthpiece and time how long the limewater takes to go cloudy. Then exercise, put in fresh limewater and repeat. A shorter time means more carbon dioxide is being breathed out.
  • Either limewater or hydrogencarbonate indicator can be named as the indicator in this practical.

Before you begin

  • Respiration produces carbon dioxide.
  • Gas exchange in the alveoli.
  • Control variables in a fair test.
1

The method

  1. When drawing, connect the mouthpiece (or T piece) to both tubes; in each tube put one long tube into the liquid and one short tube above it.

    Why: Drawing marks are for the correct layout and long/short tubes the right way round.

  2. Describe breathing in and out through the mouthpiece.

    Why: This is the first method mark.

  3. Say which tube receives exhaled air and which receives inhaled air.

    Why: Schemes credit exhaling through one named tube and inhaling through the other.

  4. Give a fair test step: time how long until cloudy, or use the same number of breaths, and replace the limewater.

    Why: Timing and control earn marks.

  5. Compare the results and link to respiration.

    Why: Explaining needs more CO2 in exhaled air, faster change, and the reason (respiration).

  6. Control how much air passes: use the same number of breaths (for example 10) or the same time (for example one minute) for each test.

    Why: A fixed amount of breathing is a mark in itself and makes the comparison fair.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Drawing both tubes dipping into the liquid in one flask, so liquid is sucked into the mouth.
  • ✗
    Saying inhaled air has no carbon dioxide.
  • ✗
    Saying the lungs make carbon dioxide instead of body cells in respiration.
  • ✗
    Describing a method with no timing or no fair comparison.
  • ✗
    Saying limewater turns 'colourless' or 'blue' instead of cloudy.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Explain the difference between the results: inhaled air versus exhaled air in limewater.
  2. What will be seen? Limewater with exhaled air goes cloudy faster.
  3. Why? Exhaled air contains more carbon dioxide.
  4. Where does that extra carbon dioxide come from? Respiration in body cells.
  5. Three marks: result, which air has more CO2, and the reason.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Exhaled air contains more carbon dioxide than inhaled air (about 4% compared with 0.04%). So the limewater with exhaled air turns cloudy more quickly, in less time. The extra carbon dioxide is made by respiration in body cells.

How the marks are earned

One mark for exhaled air having more carbon dioxide, one for the limewater taking less time to go cloudy with exhaled air, and one for respiration as the source of the carbon dioxide.
