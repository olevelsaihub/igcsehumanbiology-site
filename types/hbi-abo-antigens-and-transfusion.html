Use ABO antigens and antibodies to choose donors and explain clumping

Antigens and antibodies of each blood group, which donors suit which recipients, counting compatible people, and why mismatches cause agglutination.

Knowledge card

Use ABO antigens and antibodies to choose donors and explain clumping

Written
  • The four ABO blood groups are A, B, AB and O.
  • Group A red cells carry antigen A and the plasma has anti-B antibodies. Group B has antigen B and anti-A antibodies.
  • Group AB has both antigens and neither antibody. Group O has no antigens but both anti-A and anti-B antibodies.
  • If donor red cells carry an antigen that matches the recipient's antibodies, the antibodies bind and the red cells clump together. This is agglutination.
  • The clumps can block blood vessels and the transfusion can kill the patient, so donor blood must always be grouped and matched.
  • Group O blood can be given to every group because it has no antigens; group AB can receive every group because it has no anti-A or anti-B antibodies.
  • Antigens on red cells are made of carbohydrate and protein. Enzymes such as carbohydrases and proteases could digest and remove them, turning A, B or AB blood into group O blood.
  • Making group O blood this way means fewer donors are needed, blood is always available, and there is less risk of giving the wrong group.

Before you begin

  • Antibodies bind to specific antigens.
  • Antigens are molecules such as proteins or carbohydrates on cell surfaces.
  • Enzymes are specific: proteases digest proteins and carbohydrases digest carbohydrates.
1

The method

  1. Write a mini table: group, antigens on red cells, antibodies in plasma.

    Why: Every answer in this type comes from this table.

  2. For each recipient, check the donor's antigens against the recipient's antibodies.

    Why: A donor is safe only if the recipient has no antibody against any donor antigen.

  3. List every safe donor group for each recipient, including the recipient's own group.

    Why: Marks are lost if a safe group is left out or an unsafe one is added.

  4. When asked about a mismatch, use the chain: antigen, antibody binds, agglutination, blocked vessels, possibly death.

    Why: Each link is a separate scheme point.

  5. For a calculation, add the percentages of the groups that can safely receive the blood, then multiply by the total and divide by 100.

    Why: Method marks are given for the added percentage and the working.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Saying group O has antigen O.
  • ✗
    Saying a mismatch makes the blood 'clot'; the correct word is agglutination or clumping.
  • ✗
    Thinking the donor's antibodies are the main problem instead of the recipient's antibodies meeting the donor's antigens.
  • ✗
    Leaving out O as a safe donor for group A or B, or leaving out the recipient's own group.
  • ✗
    In the calculation, adding the groups that can donate to B instead of the groups that can receive B.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. I make a mini table. A: antigen A, anti-B. B: antigen B, anti-A. AB: antigens A and B, no antibodies. O: no antigens, anti-A and anti-B.
  2. Recipient A has anti-B. So it cannot take any blood with antigen B. Safe: A and O.
  3. Recipient B has anti-A. So it cannot take antigen A. Safe: B and O.
  4. Recipient AB has no anti-A or anti-B antibodies, so it can take A, B, AB and O.
  5. Recipient O has both antibodies, so any antigen would cause clumping. Safe: O only.
  6. I write one line for each recipient with a short reason.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A person with group A can receive group A or group O, because they have anti-B antibodies, which would make B antigens clump.

A person with group B can receive group B or group O, because their anti-A antibodies would react with A antigens.

A person with group AB can receive all groups (A, B, AB and O), because they have no anti-A or anti-B antibodies.

A person with group O can receive only group O, because they have both anti-A and anti-B antibodies, and O red cells have no antigens.

How the marks are earned

One mark for each recipient group with the correct full list of donors: A gets A and O; B gets B and O; AB gets every group; O gets only O. The reasons support the answer but the marks are for the correct donor lists.
