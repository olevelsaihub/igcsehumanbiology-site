Knowledge card

Explain how alveoli are adapted for gas exchange and effects of damage

Written
  • Alveoli have a very large total surface area, so much gas can diffuse at once.
  • The alveolus wall and the capillary wall are each one cell thick, so the diffusion distance is short.
  • The inside of each alveolus is moist, so gases dissolve before they diffuse.
  • A dense network of capillaries and constant breathing keep a steep concentration gradient for oxygen and carbon dioxide.
  • Air in the alveoli has less oxygen, more carbon dioxide and more water vapour than outside air, and it is warmer.
  • In emphysema the alveolus walls break down. There is less surface area, so less oxygen enters the blood. Cells do less aerobic respiration, so the person is breathless and tired.
  • In emphysema the small alveoli join into larger air spaces. These have a smaller surface area to volume ratio, so gas exchange is less efficient.
  • During exercise, more water is lost in sweat, so the kidneys make less urine. (This idea can appear in a gap-fill about the body during exercise.)

Before you begin

  • Diffusion down a concentration gradient
  • Aerobic respiration needs oxygen and releases energy
  • Surface area to volume ratio
1

The method

  1. For features, list separate adaptations: large surface area, thin walls, moist lining, many capillaries.

    Why: Each different feature scores one mark; repeating one idea in new words scores once.

  2. Use comparative words: large surface area, short distance, good blood supply.

    Why: Vague words such as 'big alveoli' do not show the adaptation.

  3. For damage, build a chain: walls break, less surface area, less diffusion of oxygen, less oxygen to cells, less aerobic respiration, less energy, tiredness or breathlessness.

    Why: Explain questions give one mark for each link in this chain.

  4. For alveolar air, use 'more' or 'less' for each gas.

    Why: Each comparison is one mark.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Writing 'thin cell walls'; animal cells have no cell wall, so say 'thin walls' or 'one cell thick'.
  • ✗
    Saying emphysema means 'no oxygen' instead of less oxygen diffusing into the blood.
  • ✗
    Saying alveoli have 'a large volume' instead of a large surface area.
  • ✗
    Stopping at 'less gas exchange' without linking to less respiration and less energy.
  • ✗
    Saying the person makes 'no energy'; respiration releases energy, it does not make it.
  • ✗
    Saying that emphysema gives the alveoli a 'bigger surface area' because the air spaces are bigger; the surface area to volume ratio becomes smaller.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. The diagram shows that in emphysema the alveoli walls have broken down into bigger spaces.
  2. Fewer, larger spaces mean less surface area for gas exchange.
  3. So less oxygen diffuses into the blood and less carbon dioxide is removed.
  4. Body cells get less oxygen, so they carry out less aerobic respiration and release less energy.
  5. The person feels tired, is short of breath, and cannot be very active.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

In emphysema the walls of the alveoli are damaged and break down. This reduces the surface area for gas exchange. Less oxygen diffuses into the blood and less carbon dioxide is removed. Cells receive less oxygen, so there is less aerobic respiration and less energy is released. The person is short of breath and tired, and cannot do much exercise.

How the marks are earned

Marks go to separate ideas: damaged alveolus walls, less surface area, less gas exchange into the blood, less aerobic respiration, breathlessness, and tiredness or low energy. Any four earn full marks; this answer gives six.
