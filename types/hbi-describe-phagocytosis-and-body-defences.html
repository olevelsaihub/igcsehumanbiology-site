Knowledge card

Describe phagocytosis, antibody production and barriers to pathogens

Written
  • Phagocytosis is the process where a phagocyte (a white blood cell) engulfs a pathogen.
  • The phagocyte flows around the pathogen and encloses it in a vacuole inside the cell.
  • Lysosomes contain digestive enzymes. They join with the vacuole and the enzymes digest (break down) the pathogen.
  • Lymphocytes defend the body in another way: they produce antibodies, and some release antitoxins that neutralise toxins.
  • Physical barriers such as skin, mucus and cilia stop pathogens getting in. Chemical barriers such as stomach acid and enzymes in tears kill pathogens, slow their growth or damage their cell membranes.
  • Elderly people often have a weaker immune system, and crowded indoor spaces with poor air flow let pathogens spread more easily.
  • Barriers are the first line of defence. If pathogens get past them, phagocytes act as the second line of defence; they attack any pathogen, not one particular type.

Before you begin

  • Enzymes break down large molecules into small ones.
  • Pathogens include bacteria and viruses and cause disease.
  • Cell parts such as the cell membrane and vacuoles.
1

The method

  1. Name the cell and the process: phagocyte, phagocytosis.

    Why: The name of the process or cell is often its own mark.

  2. Describe the stages in order: engulf, enclose in a vacuole, enzymes from lysosomes digest, remains removed.

    Why: Schemes give one mark per stage in the right order.

  3. Read the labels on the diagram and use them, for example 'lysosome (contains digestive enzymes)'.

    Why: The diagram tells you which words the examiner expects.

  4. For 'another way', switch to lymphocytes: antibodies or antitoxins.

    Why: Repeating engulfing scores nothing when it is already shown.

  5. For barriers, name the barrier and say what it does (blocks entry, or kills or slows the pathogen).

    Why: The action is what earns the mark for a chemical barrier.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Saying phagocytes 'eat' bacteria with no mention of enzymes digesting them.
  • ✗
    Saying antibodies digest bacteria; enzymes digest, antibodies bind to antigens.
  • ✗
    Writing that lymphocytes engulf pathogens.
  • ✗
    Choosing 'erythrocytes' from a word box for the engulfing cell.
  • ✗
    Naming a barrier but not saying how it protects the body.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. I look at the diagram. The bacterium is engulfed and is now inside a vacuole. A lysosome with digestive enzymes is next to it.
  2. Stage 1 is the arrow from the lysosome to the vacuole holding the bacterium. So at stage 1 the lysosome joins with that vacuole.
  3. Stage 2 shows the bacterium broken into small pieces. The enzymes from the lysosome are digesting it.
  4. Stage 3 shows a vesicle of small pieces leaving the cell. The left-over parts of the bacterium are being removed from the cell.
  5. I write one clear sentence for each stage.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Stage 1: The lysosome moves to the vacuole that holds the engulfed bacterium and joins with it.

Stage 2: Digestive enzymes from the lysosome break down (digest) the bacterium.

Stage 3: The waste pieces of the digested bacterium are released out of the white blood cell.

How the marks are earned

One mark per stage: stage 1 for the lysosome joining with the vacuole around the bacterium; stage 2 for enzymes digesting the bacterium; stage 3 for the remains being expelled from the cell.
