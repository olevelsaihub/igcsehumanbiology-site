Describe how to carry out a food test, including DCPIP for vitamin C

Writing the method for Benedict's, iodine, biuret, emulsion or DCPIP tests on foods, urine, plasma or tubing contents, including safe conduct and a DCPIP comparison of two juices.

Type 4 Biological molecules HBI-FOOD-TEST-PROCEDURE

★★★★★ · 13 of 36 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Medium.

Ordered recall of steps with a link from colour to conclusion. Four tests with different reagents and colours must be kept apart. No calculation; DCPIP comparison only counts drops. Samples change: plasma, urine, yoghurt, meat, tubing contents. Combines practical method, safety and fair testing.

Knowledge card

Describe how to carry out a food test, including DCPIP for vitamin C

Written
  • Reducing sugar (glucose): add Benedict's solution and heat in a water bath; blue turns green, yellow, orange or brick red if sugar is present.
  • Starch: add iodine solution; brown (orange-brown) turns blue-black if starch is present.
  • Protein: add biuret solution; blue turns lilac (purple) if protein is present.
  • Lipid: shake the food with ethanol, then add water; a milky white emulsion shows lipid.
  • Vitamin C: add the juice drop by drop to a fixed volume of blue DCPIP; count the drops needed to make it colourless. Fewer drops means more vitamin C.
  • Solid food is cut up or ground and mixed with water before testing.
  • Wear eye protection, and use a water bath rather than a naked flame to heat Benedict's tests.
  • Exam DCPIP method: put a measured volume of juice in a test tube and add blue DCPIP one drop at a time until the blue colour stays; record the volume added. Repeat with the same volume of the other (or boiled) juice and compare: more DCPIP decolourised means more vitamin C.
  • To show a product contains no enzyme, test it with biuret, because enzymes are proteins: it stays blue if no enzyme is present.
  • Glucose in urine can also be tested with a glucose test strip: dip it into the urine and compare its colour with the chart.

The method

  1. Name the correct test and reagent for the nutrient asked.

    Why: The reagent is a mark in every scheme.

  2. Say how to prepare the sample: put it in a test tube, grind or chop solids, add water.

    Why: Longer questions credit the sample step.

  3. Give the key action: heat in a water bath (Benedict's), shake with ethanol then add water (lipid), add drop by drop (DCPIP).

    Why: This is the step that most often separates full marks from partial marks.

  4. Give the colour change for a positive result, and what you see if it is negative.

    Why: The positive colour is always worth a mark.

  5. If asked 'safely' or for a comparison, add goggles, and keep volumes the same and repeat.

    Why: Safety and fair-test points fill the extra marks in 4 or 5-mark items.

  6. For a DCPIP comparison, keep the volume of juice the same, record the volume (or drops) of DCPIP, repeat for a mean, and compare the volumes.

    Why: Design questions give marks for the measurement, the repeat and the comparison, not just for naming DCPIP.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Heating the biuret or iodine test, or forgetting to heat Benedict's.
  • ✗
    Saying the Benedict's colour is 'red' for protein, or 'purple' for sugar.
  • ✗
    Adding water before ethanol in the emulsion test.
  • ✗
    Writing 'add Benedict's to glucose' instead of to the sample being tested.
  • ✗
    In DCPIP comparisons, not keeping the volume of DCPIP the same or not counting drops.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. The question asks how to test plasma for protein. The protein test uses biuret solution.
  2. First I need the sample: put some plasma into a clean test tube.
  3. Then add biuret reagent. No heating is needed.
  4. Then give the result: the blue colour turns lilac or purple.
  5. Finally say what this means: protein is present. I can add the negative result too: it stays blue if there is no protein.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Put about 2 cm3 of the plasma into a clean test tube. Add an equal volume of biuret reagent and shake gently; do not heat. If the blue colour changes to lilac (purple), this shows that protein is present in the plasma. If the solution stays blue, there is no protein.

How the marks are earned

Mark 1: the plasma sample goes into a test tube. Mark 2: biuret reagent is added. Mark 3: the colour change from blue to lilac or purple. Mark 4: this colour means protein is present (or staying blue means none). The last mark is linked to the colour change mark.
