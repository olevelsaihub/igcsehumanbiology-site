Knowledge card

Explain systolic, diastolic and changing pressure around the circulation

Written
  • Systolic pressure is the highest pressure of blood on the artery walls, when the ventricles contract and push blood out.
  • Diastolic pressure is the lowest pressure in the arteries, when the ventricles relax between beats.
  • In the left ventricle pressure swings from very high (about 120 mmHg) to near zero as it contracts and relaxes.
  • In arteries the pressure is high and rises and falls with each beat (the pulse); the swings get smaller further from the heart.
  • In capillaries the pressure falls steadily and does not pulse, because capillary walls have no muscle or elastic tissue. Pressure falls along a capillary because it is far from the heart, the narrow vessels resist flow, and some fluid leaves as tissue fluid. In veins it is lowest.
  • Blood always flows from a region of higher pressure to a region of lower pressure, for example from an artery to a vein.
  • In a dialysis machine, blood is taken from an artery, where it is at high pressure, and returned to a vein, where it is at low pressure, so it flows that way through the tubing.

Before you begin

  • Structure of the heart and the order of chambers and vessels.
  • Structure of arteries, veins and capillaries.
  • Reading values from a graph.
1

The method

  1. Match each region of the graph to a structure: large swings down to zero = ventricle; high swings that stay above about 70 = arteries; smooth fall = capillaries; lowest = veins.

    Why: Region-naming marks depend on reading the shape, not just the height.

  2. For a rising and falling region, link the rise to the ventricle contracting and the fall to it relaxing.

    Why: Contraction and relaxation are separate marks.

  3. Say where the blood goes: forced out of the heart into the aorta.

    Why: Schemes credit the destination as well as the pressure.

  4. For capillaries, say there is no pulse because the walls have no muscle or elastic tissue, and pressure keeps dropping.

    Why: These are the ideas credited for steady falling pressure.

  5. For flow direction, compare the two pressures and say blood moves from high to low.

    Why: The comparison is what earns the pressure marks.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Saying pressure rises when the atria contract, instead of the ventricles.
  • ✗
    Swapping systolic and diastolic.
  • ✗
    Naming the region with the large swings 'arteries' instead of the heart (ventricle).
  • ✗
    Saying the pressure 'stops' in capillaries instead of falling.
  • ✗
    Saying blood flows from vein to artery in a dialysis machine.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. I look at region 1. Pressure rises to about 120 mmHg and falls almost to 0, again and again.
  2. Only a heart chamber can fall almost to zero, so region 1 is the heart, the left ventricle.
  3. Why does it rise? The left ventricle muscle contracts. This forces blood out of the heart into the aorta at high pressure.
  4. Why does it fall? The ventricle relaxes, so the pressure inside it drops.
  5. This repeats with every heartbeat, so the graph goes up and down.
  6. I write each idea as a separate sentence to collect five marks.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Region 1 is the heart (the left ventricle). When the muscle of the left ventricle contracts, it squeezes the blood and forces it out of the heart into the aorta. This makes the pressure very high. Then the ventricle relaxes, so the pressure in it falls to almost zero. This contracting and relaxing happens with every heartbeat, so the pressure keeps rising and falling.

How the marks are earned

Marks go for: saying it is the heart; the left ventricle contracting; blood being pushed out of the heart or into the aorta; the pressure being high; the ventricle relaxing; and the pressure dropping. Five of these six give full marks.
