State what antibiotics treat and explain how resistance develops

What antibiotics are and where they come from, why they fail against viruses and fungi, and selection of resistant strains such as MRSA and its consequences.

Knowledge card

State what antibiotics treat and explain how resistance develops

Written
  • Antibiotics are substances made by microorganisms, mainly fungi (such as Penicillium) and some bacteria.
  • Antibiotics kill bacteria or stop them growing. They are used to treat bacterial diseases such as pneumonia, gonorrhoea, cholera and TB.
  • Antibiotics do not work against viruses (such as Ebola or influenza), because viruses live inside our cells and lack the structures antibiotics attack. They do not treat fungal infections such as athlete's foot either.
  • Resistance starts with a random mutation in a bacterium's DNA (or a plasmid with a resistance gene).
  • When an antibiotic is used, non-resistant bacteria die, but resistant bacteria survive, reproduce and pass on the resistance gene. Over many generations most of the population becomes resistant.
  • Overuse of antibiotics, and patients not finishing the course, help resistant bacteria to be selected.
  • MRSA is a strain of Staphylococcus aureus resistant to many antibiotics. Infections are hard to treat, spread easily in hospitals, and need other, often stronger, antibiotics.

Before you begin

  • Bacteria are cells; viruses are not cells and reproduce inside host cells.
  • Mutations are random changes in DNA that can be inherited.
  • Natural selection: individuals with useful variations survive and reproduce.
1

The method

  1. Check the type of pathogen in the question: bacterium, virus or fungus.

    Why: 'Cannot be treated' questions earn one mark for naming the pathogen type.

  2. State what antibiotics act on: bacteria only.

    Why: The second mark links antibiotics to bacteria and not to viruses or fungi.

  3. For resistance, write the sequence: mutation, antibiotic kills non-resistant bacteria, resistant ones survive, reproduce, pass on the gene, population becomes resistant.

    Why: Each step in the sequence is a separate mark.

  4. Add a cause (overuse, not finishing the course) and a consequence (harder to treat, another antibiotic needed, spreads).

    Why: 'Discuss' and 'concern' questions credit causes and effects.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Saying bacteria become 'immune' to an antibiotic. Immunity is about antibodies; bacteria become resistant.
  • ✗
    Saying the antibiotic causes the mutation. The mutation is random and happens first.
  • ✗
    Writing that antibiotics kill viruses, or that antibodies and antibiotics are the same thing.
  • ✗
    Naming an antibiotic such as penicillin when asked for a source; the source is a fungus or bacterium.
  • ✗
    Stopping at 'resistant bacteria survive' without saying they reproduce and pass on resistance.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. 'Explain why it is not possible', 2 marks: two linked ideas.
  2. What causes athlete's foot? A fungus.
  3. What do antibiotics act on? Bacteria only.
  4. Link them: antibiotics do not kill fungi, so they cannot cure athlete's foot.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Athlete's foot is caused by a fungus, not a bacterium. Antibiotics only kill bacteria; they have no effect on fungi, so they cannot cure athlete's foot.

How the marks are earned

One mark for saying a fungus causes athlete's foot. One mark for saying antibiotics work only on bacteria, or that they do not kill fungi.
