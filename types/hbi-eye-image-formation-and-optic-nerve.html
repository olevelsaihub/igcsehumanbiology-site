Knowledge card

Show how an image forms on the retina and reaches the brain

Written
  • Light rays from an object are refracted (bent inwards) first at the cornea and then again at the lens.
  • The rays from the top and bottom of the object cross over inside the eye, behind the lens.
  • The rays meet on the retina. The image there is upside down (inverted) and smaller than the object.
  • Receptor cells (rods and cones) in the retina turn light into electrical impulses.
  • Sensory neurones in the optic nerve carry these impulses from the retina to the brain.
  • The brain interprets the impulses so we see the object the right way up.
  • If the optic nerve is damaged (for example by glaucoma), fewer or no impulses reach the brain, so vision becomes blurred, the image is not formed, or the person becomes blind.
  • The optic nerve can also carry impulses from the brain back to the eye muscles (iris and ciliary muscles), but in an image question describe the route from the rods and cones to the brain.

Before you begin

  • The parts of the eye and the path light takes through them.
  • Refraction: light bends when it passes into a different transparent material.
  • Sensory neurones carry impulses to the CNS.
1

The method

  1. Use a ruler. Put arrowheads on the rays to show light travels into the eye.

    Why: Arrowheads showing the direction of light can earn a mark.

  2. Bend each ray inwards where it enters the cornea.

    Why: Bending at the cornea is a separate mark; bending outwards scores zero.

  3. Bend each ray inwards again at the lens.

    Why: Bending at the lens is a separate mark.

  4. Make the rays cross after the lens and meet exactly on the retina, not before or behind it.

    Why: Crossing inside the eye and meeting on the retina each earn credit.

  5. Draw the image on the retina upside down.

    Why: The inverted image is a separate mark.

  6. For the optic nerve, say it carries impulses from the retina to the brain.

    Why: Two marks: what it carries, and from where to where.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Drawing straight rays that do not bend at the cornea.
  • ✗
    Making the rays cross before the lens or meet in front of the retina.
  • ✗
    Drawing the image the right way up.
  • ✗
    Saying the optic nerve carries 'light' or 'the image' to the brain. It carries electrical impulses.
  • ✗
    Saying the optic nerve sends messages from the brain to the eye in an image-formation question.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. The diagram shows an arrow (a near object) in front of the eye with two horizontal rays reaching the cornea. It is worth 5 marks.
  2. So I expect marks for: arrowheads, bending at the cornea, bending at the lens, crossing, and an upside-down image on the retina.
  3. I add arrowheads to both rays pointing towards the eye.
  4. Where each ray meets the cornea, I bend it slightly inwards towards the centre.
  5. Where each ray meets the lens, I bend it inwards again, more steeply.
  6. The top ray goes down and the bottom ray goes up, so they cross over between the lens and the retina.
  7. Both rays end on the retina at the back. I draw a small upside-down arrow there between the two end points.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

[Drawing] Both rays have arrowheads pointing into the eye. Each ray bends inwards at the cornea and bends inwards again at the lens. The rays cross over inside the eyeball after the lens and continue to the retina. A small, upside-down arrow is drawn on the retina where the rays meet it.

How the marks are earned

One mark each for: arrowheads showing light going into the eye; bending at the cornea; bending at the lens; rays crossing inside the eye; an inverted image on the retina. Rays that cross in front of the lens or bend outwards do not earn the bending or crossing marks.
