Knowledge card

Identify arteries, veins and capillaries and relate their structure to function

Written
  • There are three main types of blood vessel: arteries, veins and capillaries. Small arteries are arterioles and small veins are venules.
  • Arteries carry blood away from the heart at high pressure. Their walls are thick, with a lot of muscle and elastic tissue, to withstand the pressure. The walls stretch and recoil with each beat, which we feel as the pulse.
  • An artery has a small, round lumen that keeps its shape. The narrow lumen helps keep the pressure high.
  • Muscle in the walls of arteries and arterioles can contract. This narrows the lumen, leaves less space for the blood, and raises blood pressure (this is how angiotensin II works).
  • Veins carry blood back to the heart at low pressure. They have a large lumen, thin walls with little muscle and elastic tissue, and often an irregular, flattened shape in section.
  • Veins have valves that stop blood flowing backwards. Among blood vessels, valves are found only in veins, so a valve in a picture tells you it is a vein. The valve flaps point the way the blood flows.
  • Capillaries have walls one cell thick with small pores, so the diffusion distance is short. Their lumen is so narrow that red cells pass one at a time, slowly and close to the wall.
  • Capillaries are where materials are exchanged between blood and body cells: oxygen and nutrients such as glucose go to the cells, and carbon dioxide and other wastes are taken away.
  • The aorta carries oxygenated blood from the left ventricle to the body organs and keeps the blood at high pressure.

Before you begin

  • The heart pumps blood out at high pressure.
  • Factors that affect the rate of diffusion.
  • The general route of the circulation, and which vessels go to and from the heart.

The method

  1. To identify a vessel, look at the wall thickness, the lumen size and shape, any valves, and whether the wall is one cell thick.

    Why: Thick wall and small lumen means artery; thin wall, large lumen or valve means vein; one-cell wall means capillary.

  2. When asked for a reason for your choice, give a feature you can see in the picture.

    Why: The mark is for visible evidence, such as 'valve present', not for what the vessel does.

  3. When explaining a difference, write the feature, then 'so', then the function linked to pressure or exchange.

    Why: Explain questions need the reason, such as thick muscular wall so it can withstand high pressure.

  4. For a comparison, write about both vessels with comparative words: thicker, smaller lumen, no valves.

    Why: A statement about only one vessel, with no comparison, often does not earn the mark.

  5. For capillaries, link each feature to faster diffusion: one-cell wall and pores give a short distance; a narrow lumen slows the cells.

    Why: Each feature and its effect on diffusion can be a separate mark.

  6. For a function question, say what is carried, from where to where, and the pressure for an artery.

    Why: Function marks need a named substance or a direction, not just 'carries blood'.

  7. For a drawing, keep the same style as the given vessel and show a wider lumen, thinner walls and valves; for flow direction, draw the arrow the way the valve flaps point.

    Why: Each clear feature in the drawing earns a mark, and labels or arrows on their own do not.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Writing 'arteries have thick walls' without linking to high pressure.
  • ✗
    Saying veins have valves 'to pump blood'.
  • ✗
    Comparing only one vessel, for example 'vein X has a wide lumen', with no comparison word.
  • ✗
    Giving 'carries blood to the heart' as the reason for identifying a vein in a picture, instead of a feature you can see such as a valve.
  • ✗
    Naming capillaries or veins when asked which vessel can narrow to raise blood pressure; only arteries and arterioles have enough muscle.
  • ✗
    Drawing the vein the same width as the artery, or drawing more than one line from a vessel in a matching task.

Worked explanation

  1. Earlier parts showed X is the aorta (an artery) and Y is the vena cava (a vein). The question wants differences in structure, explained.
  2. The aorta carries blood straight from the left ventricle at high pressure. So its wall is thicker, with more muscle and elastic tissue, to withstand that pressure.
  3. Its lumen is narrower, which keeps the pressure high.
  4. The vena cava carries blood at low pressure. Its lumen is wider, so blood flows back easily at low pressure.
  5. It has valves to stop blood flowing backwards.
  6. That gives four explained differences for 3 marks.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Blood vessel X, the aorta, has a thicker wall with more muscle and elastic fibres than Y. This lets it withstand the high pressure of blood leaving the heart and keeps the blood moving. X has a narrower lumen, which helps keep the pressure high. Y, the vena cava, has a wider lumen because the blood in it is at low pressure, and Y has valves to stop blood flowing backwards.

How the marks are earned

Any three explained differences earn the marks: a thicker, more muscular or elastic wall for high pressure; a narrower lumen to keep pressure high; for Y, less muscle and slower flow, a wider lumen for low pressure, or valves stopping backflow.
