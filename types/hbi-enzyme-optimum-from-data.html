Knowledge card

Read an enzyme optimum from data and judge how reliable it is

Written
  • The optimum is the temperature or pH at which the enzyme works fastest.
  • If the data show rate or product made, the optimum is at the highest value. If the data show time taken, the optimum is at the shortest time.
  • Always quote the optimum with its unit (°C) or as a pH value.
  • Human gut enzymes have an optimum near 37 °C; enzymes from bacteria in hot places can have much higher optima.
  • The true optimum may lie between two tested values. Wide gaps between values, no repeats and no means make the optimum less certain.
  • To find the optimum more precisely, test smaller steps (for example every 2 °C) around the best value and repeat each test.
  • Validity: only the factor being tested should change. If the time left, the volumes or the temperature differ between tubes, the comparison is not fair.
  • Accuracy: judging by eye when a mixture 'goes clear' is subjective and different students may judge it differently; a colorimeter would be better. Too short a time may also stop a tube reaching its end point.
  • When a sample is heated, cooled, acidified or made alkaline, the untreated sample at room temperature may show the most activity.

Before you begin

  • Enzyme activity depends on temperature and pH.
  • Reading tables and graphs.
  • Validity, accuracy and repeatability in investigations.
1

The method

  1. Check what the data measure: rate or amount of product (high is good) or time taken (low is good).

    Why: Mixing these up gives the wrong optimum.

  2. Find the best value and read off its temperature or pH, with units.

    Why: One mark for the correct reading.

  3. For comparisons, quote data for both enzymes: which is faster, which has a higher optimum, which works over a wider range.

    Why: Each comparison point needs both enzymes.

  4. When asked to judge or discuss, look at the gaps between tested values, the lack of repeats and other variables.

    Why: Evaluation marks come from limits of the data, not from biology recall.

  5. Say the optimum could lie between tested values and suggest smaller intervals or repeats.

    Why: This idea is credited in almost every 'discuss' item.

  6. For 'comment on validity and accuracy', give at least one point about fairness (what was or was not kept the same) and at least one about how well the end point was measured.

    Why: These comments are marked in two groups, so both groups are needed for full marks.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Choosing the pH with the longest time as the optimum.
  • ✗
    Giving a number with no unit.
  • ✗
    Saying the optimum is 'exactly' a tested value when the steps are 10 °C apart.
  • ✗
    In a comparison, describing only one enzyme.
  • ✗
    Suggesting 'repeat the experiment' without saying smaller intervals near the peak.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. The table gives time taken to digest starch at pH 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10.
  2. The data are times, not rates. The enzyme works fastest when the time is shortest.
  3. Times: pH 2 = 60 min, pH 4 = 45 min, pH 6 = 20 min, pH 8 = 35 min, pH 10 = 55 min. The shortest is 20 minutes.
  4. So the optimum pH is 6.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

pH 6

How the marks are earned

One mark for pH 6, the pH with the shortest digestion time (20 minutes). Choosing pH 2 (the longest time) shows the student has read time as if it were rate.

Calculation check

  • min([60, 45, 20, 35, 55]) = 20
